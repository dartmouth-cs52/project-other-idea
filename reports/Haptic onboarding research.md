# Haptic must earn its script tag

The team's three working beliefs each fail in the form stated, and correcting them changes the order of Haptic's onboarding more than its contents. **The script tag is the right production install and the wrong first ask**: it is the first step that needs an engineer, a Content Security Policy change and a security review; one comparable snippet product reported almost half its signups still uninstalled after a month (a figure seen only in a search snippet); and five self-serve in-app guidance products, including Haptic's closest competitor, let a non-developer run the product on their own logged-in app through a Chrome extension before any code ships. **The Crisp/Hugo wizard transfers in shape but not in substance**, because Crisp builds its proof from a public website and Haptic's subject is a logged-in app that no crawler in the evidence can read, Haptic's own included. **The paper the team cited tested "memory in the prompt" directly and found it bought nothing for a strong model** (42.0% task success and 8.41 model calls with the map, against 44.4% and 8.16 without); its gain came from a deterministic executor that skips model calls, on 27 Microsoft Office tasks, and its authors name the web as the case they do not handle. Haptic as built is the configuration that test found unhelpful: a static map in the prompt and one model call per step. The speed levers the evidence points to instead are a non-reasoning model with the pointer target streamed first, and a per-customer store of verified routes that is checked against the live page and lets repeat steps skip the model; neither has been measured on a product like Haptic. Those routes come from an admin demonstrating flows and from guided sessions, which is the "different onboarding" the team suspected. The recommended flow follows: keep the request-access gate and onboard the first handful of companies by hand, show Haptic on the buyer's own app before asking for the script, make the hand-off to an engineer a designed step with live verification, go live to the customer's own staff first, and build self-serve from what those calls reveal. The number that matters most, the share of signups who are not engineers and still get a script installed, is published nowhere, so the first cohort's own funnel is the most valuable measurement available.

**How to read the evidence.** Every claim below carries the strength the underlying research notes gave it. "Controlled" means a randomised test. "Self-reported" means a company describing its own product with no control group. "Vendor telemetry" means a tool vendor's data from its own customers, who are not a random sample. "Snippet only" means the claim was seen in a search-result summary and the page itself could not be opened. "Documentation" shows that a pattern exists, not that it works. "Inference" marks reasoning by the researchers or by this report, not a sourced fact. Competitor pages were read through a tool that summarises, so quoted wording should be re-checked before it is repeated publicly. No competitor flow was signed up for or walked through; everything said about competitors rests on their documentation and marketing pages as of 12 November 2026. Facts about Haptic's own code and website come from its repository. Each major conclusion ends with a confidence level: high, moderate, low or unknown.

## Company onboarding ends at the first real end-user answer, not at the install

### The account is the unit, and setup is not the "aha"

There is no single authoritative definition of onboarding. The practitioners cited most often agree on three points. First, onboarding starts before signup and has no fixed end: Samuel Hulick defines it as "any time there's an opportunity to increase the likelihood that users are successful" and places the start "long before they sign up" ([Intercom interview](https://www.intercom.com/blog/podcasts/user-onboarding-interview-samuel-hulick/)). Second, the measurable milestone inside it is activation, "the earliest point in your onboarding flow that, by showing your product's value, is predictive of long-term retention" ([Lenny's Newsletter](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/what-is-a-good-activation-rate)). Third, in B2B the thing that must activate is the account, not the person who signed up: Miro did not count an account as activated until two people had collaborated, and PostHog checks whether "an organization meets the activation definition within 30 days" ([Elena Verna](https://www.elenaverna.com/p/hey-b2b-i-bet-you-are-measuring-activation); [PostHog handbook](https://posthog.com/handbook/growth/growth-engineering/per-product-activation)). Verna, quoting the Reforge definition, splits activation into setup, aha and habit and names the common error directly: "Many companies excel at innovating in setup and mistakenly want to count setup as their value delivery, or aha moment" ([Elena Verna](https://www.elenaverna.com/p/hey-b2b-i-bet-you-are-measuring-activation)).

Applied to Haptic, a company onboarding flow is the whole account-level path from the "Request access" form on joinhaptic.com to the point where the customer's own end users are being guided and coming back. **Installing the script is setup. The aha is the buyer watching the pointer find the right control on their own app. Activation is the first real end-user question answered in production, and habit is repeat use in the following weeks.** The person who signs up for Haptic, the person who installs it and the person who approves it are often three different people, so "the user activated" and "the account activated" are different events (an inference from the definitions above). For orientation only: self-reported median activation across surveys sits between about 25% and 37% of signups, each source using its own milestone, and products sold to developers convert free users to paid at about half the rate of other software (5% against 10% within six months in a secondary analysis of OpenView's 2023 survey) ([Lenny's Newsletter](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/what-is-a-good-activation-rate); [Userpilot](https://userpilot.com/blog/saas-onboarding-benchmarks/); [boldstart](https://boldstart.vc/?p=1596)). With a handful of accounts these are not targets; the funnel should be read as a list of named companies. **Confidence: high** that this is the consensus definition. It is framework and opinion, not measurement.

### No one publishes how many signups finish a snippet install

The planning number Haptic needs most is the share of signups that complete a snippet or SDK install when the person signing up is not the person installing. The researchers searched for it across Segment, Hotjar, Mixpanel, Amplitude, Heap, FullStory, LaunchDarkly, Stripe, Algolia and Intercom and found only help pages. Two weak anchors exist. Upscope, a co-browsing product installed by snippet, wrote that "almost half did not have Upscope installed after one month" (snippet only; the post could not be opened) ([Upscope](https://blog.upscope.com/this-simple-intercom-email-trick-helped-us-double-installations)). Sentry, whose signups are developers, reported that about **70% of users on its two best platforms sent a first event within two days** in 2017 ([Sentry](https://blog.sentry.io/small-data-big-insights)). Sentry is a ceiling case, because its signups can install the product themselves. From these two points, expecting well under half of non-developer signups to reach a live install without human help is a reasonable planning assumption. It is an estimate from two weak data points, not a benchmark.

Three levers are reported to raise the install rate, in rising order of claimed effect. The first is making instructions specific to the customer's stack, which Sentry did by asking for the platform at signup ([Sentry](https://blog.sentry.io/small-data-big-insights)). The second is routing the task to the right person. Upscope asked each signup whether they could install the product themselves, showed the code to those who said yes, and gave everyone else a one-click email to their developer with Upscope copied in; it claimed this halved the share still uninstalled after a month (snippet only, no sample size, no control) ([Upscope](https://blog.upscope.com/this-simple-intercom-email-trick-helped-us-double-installations)). Intercom documents the same idea as a developer invite carrying "the minimum permissions required to install the Messenger" ([Intercom help](https://www.intercom.com/help/en/articles/16946116-new-messenger-install-experience-on-your-website)). The third is removing the manual work. PostHog reports that users of its command-line install wizard convert to paid at **14.2% against 2.6%** and reach a first event in 1.9 hours against 3.8, and DevCycle reports about three times as many users reaching SDK install after moving the install into the developer's AI coding assistant ([PostHog](https://posthog.com/newsletter/context-engineering); [DevCycle](https://blog.devcycle.com/we-rebuilt-our-onboarding-around-mcp-the-result-3x-sdk-installs/)). Both are self-reported and uncontrolled. PostHog's post names no sample, period or assignment method, and developers who choose a wizard are likely more committed than those who do not, so the gap overstates the causal effect. Amplitude is running a 50/50 test of a coding-agent install option and has published no result ([Amplitude docs](https://amplitude.com/docs/en/get-started/setup-wizard-cli)). **Confidence: moderate** on direction, because independent companies report the same thing; **low** on every magnitude.

### Value before the hard step helps; blocking helps only when the step is the value

The direction of the evidence favours letting the buyer experience something real before the install, and nearly all of it is self-reported. Chameleon, itself a snippet-installed guidance product, built a sandbox that needs no code and reported that users who explored it "reached key milestones 25% faster"; those users chose to explore, so motivation is a confound ([Chameleon](https://www.chameleon.io/shift-left/auditing-onboarding)). Twilio's redesign, which included a simulated phone that receives test messages before any carrier registration, reported "a 62% improvement in first message activation" with no baseline or sample given ([Twilio](https://www.twilio.com/en-us/blog/developers/redesigning-twilio-onboarding-experience-whats-new)). The one controlled result is Sentry's: a prominent "See Sample Event" button at the top of the install-instructions page **beat control by 12% on the share of signups that went on to send a real error** ([Sentry](https://blog.sentry.io/easy-ab-testing-with-planout)). Showing what success looks like on the waiting screen raised real installs. Every vendor in the notes that kept the install first has also added a way out of the waiting screen: Sentry's sample error, PostHog's skip action, Microsoft Clarity's public demo project. No fetched source shows a hard block with no alternative ([Sentry source](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/getsentry/sentry/master/static/app/views/onboarding/components/firstEventFooter.tsx); [PostHog source](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/PostHog/posthog/master/frontend/src/scenes/onboarding/onboardingLogic.tsx); [Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/clarity/setup-and-installation/getting-started)).

There is evidence in the other direction, and it concerns a different question. Superhuman made one setup step full-screen and interruptive and saw completion go "from 30% to over 98%"; when its onboarding call went from mandatory to optional, attendance fell from 100% to a typical 15% ([First Round Review](https://review.firstround.com/superhuman-onboarding-playbook/)). The two findings fit together once the questions are separated. Should the buyer see value before the hard step? Mostly yes. Should a step be blocking? Only when the step itself is the value and the person in front of it can perform it. A blocking "waiting for your script" screen fails the second test whenever the signer is not an engineer (inference). For Haptic, generic demo data is a weak substitute for the real thing, because the aha is "it works on my app"; no source tested a pre-install trial on the customer's own app for a product of this type. **Confidence: moderate.**

### Ask only what changes the next screen

Removing signup fields raises signups, but the better-supported finding is that questions which route the user cost little. One-click sign-in through a third-party account was associated with signup conversion 8.2 percentage points higher across 79 companies (2017, observational) ([Heap](https://heap.io/blog/good-conversion-rate-signup-flow)). Rows nudged users toward a company email and saw a "33% increase in the share of users registering with a work email" with website conversion unchanged at 5–6%; its use-case question, which changed nothing downstream, drew "Not sure" or "Other" from 25% of respondents; and its generic invite-teammates step was used by about 1% of new signups (pre/post comparison, sample sizes not reported) ([Rows](https://rows.com/blog/post/experimenting-with-questions-and-hints-in-the-user-onboarding)). Twilio's role and code-or-no-code question forks the flow and was part of its reported activation gain, though its separate contribution was not isolated ([Twilio](https://www.twilio.com/en-us/blog/developers/redesigning-twilio-onboarding-experience-whats-new)). The lesson is to ask only what the product will branch on. For Haptic that is one question: can you add a script to your app yourself? Invites work the same way. In a controlled 30-day test, Sentry let any member request an invite for a colleague and let outsiders ask to join, which together raised accepted invitations by 21%, and 71–78% of invitees became active; Rows's generic invite step was used by about 1% ([Sentry](https://blog.sentry.io/how-we-grew-sentrys-monthly-active-users-by-rethinking-invitations)). The invite that matters for Haptic is "send this to the person who can install it". **Confidence: moderate.**

### Checklists reach about a fifth of users, and the psychology cited for them is thin

In the one vendor dataset that reports it, the average onboarding checklist completion rate is **19.2%**, and in another about **23% of users** click inside a checklist launcher at all ([Userpilot](https://userpilot.com/blog/product-metrics-benchmark-report/); [Chameleon](https://chameleon.io/benchmark-report)). No public controlled test of checklist against no checklist, with activation as the outcome, was found. The psychology usually cited is weaker than vendor posts suggest. The endowed-progress study is real and peer-reviewed, but it concerned loyalty cards redeemed for a tangible reward, and its abstract says the effect depends on "the reason, if any, offered for the endowment" ([Journal of Consumer Research](https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article/32/4/504/1787425)). A 2025 meta-analysis "found no memory advantage for unfinished tasks", so the Zeigarnik effect should not be cited for checklists ([Ghibellini and Meier](https://ideas.repec.org/a/pal/palcom/v12y2025i1d10.1057_s41599-025-05000-w.html)). A meta-analysis of 32 randomised web-survey experiments found that a constant progress indicator "does not significantly help reduce drop-offs" and that indicators which start slowly increase drop-off ([Villar, Callegaro and Yang](https://research.google/pubs/where-am-i-a-meta-analysis-of-experiments-on-the-effects-of-progress-indicators-for-web-surveys/)). Sentry found in 2019 that its onboarding widget, "a list of TODOs rather than a guided tour of the product", had low engagement and replaced it with contextual help that raised its first-event rate by almost 6% ([Sentry](https://blog.sentry.io/growing-adoption-building-modern-clippy)). Vendor guidance is three to five items, with no data behind it ([ProductLed](https://productled.com/blog/product-led-user-onboarding-experience)).

Three design consequences follow, all inferences. A checklist is a secondary aid for the motivated minority, so the main path has to be the flow itself. A pre-ticked first item is legitimate when it has a stated reason, which is the condition under which the head-start effect held. And a list whose first open item is "wait for an engineer" starts slowly, which is the pattern that raised drop-off in surveys; items the buyer can finish alone belong first. The transfer from surveys to onboarding is the researchers' reasoning, not a tested result. **Confidence: moderate** that a checklist is secondary; **low** on the right number of items.

### Human help, staged exposure and the security review belong inside the flow

The evidence on gating is founder testimony, and it is consistent. Superhuman's head of onboarding says "every founder should spend time manually onboarding their earliest customers" and reports human-led onboarding at "nearly 2x" the activation of self-serve, while noting selection bias; replacing it in-product took three years ([First Round Review](https://review.firstround.com/superhuman-onboarding-playbook/)). Arcade ran a waitlist until it "got to know our first one hundred customers very well", learned that users "were confused about the first action that they needed to take", and opened self-serve when it could no longer keep up with leads ([Arcade](https://arcade.software/post/going-self-service)). Paul Graham's name for doing the install with the customer is the "Collison installation", after Stripe's founders: "Right then, give me your laptop" ([Paul Graham](https://paulgraham.com/ds.html)). Survey data point the same way and are correlational: companies that reached out to users "retained them 2-3x better", and **80% of free-trial products add human touchpoints when an enterprise user enters the trial** ([OpenView via PR Newswire](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/openviews-third-annual-product-benchmarks-report-reveals-plg-companies-2x-more-likely-to-grow-quickly-301562090.html); [ChartMogul](https://chartmogul.com/reports/saas-conversion-report/)). No benchmark for waitlist conversion or decay was found.

A script inside a customer's app runs with that app's privileges, so a reviewer treats it as code the customer is shipping. The documentation of mature vendors shows what reviewers expect: exact Content Security Policy entries (the browser rule list naming which hosts a page may load scripts from and send data to), a pinnable version, masking done in the browser before anything leaves it, and a rollout that starts with internal users ([PostHog docs](https://posthog.com/docs/libraries/js/security); [FullStory help](https://help.fullstory.com/hc/en-us/articles/360020623574); [Intercom help](https://www.intercom.com/help/en/articles/7321743-best-practices-for-testing-intercom-and-going-live)). Payment pages carry an extra burden: PCI DSS requirement 6.4.3 says every script on a payment page must be authorised, integrity-checked and listed in a written inventory with a justification, which matters to any guide that points at billing screens (wording from a practitioner summary, not the standard itself) ([Report URI](https://blog.report-uri.com/pci-dss-4-0-1-and-requirements-6-4-3-and-11-6-1-what-changed-for-payment-page-scripts/)). AI support agents have converged on a similar caution: train on the customer's content, test in a playground, test in bulk against real past questions, then release to a limited audience, and Intercom and Decagon both shipped formal evaluation and release tooling in 2025–2026 ([Intercom](https://www.intercom.com/blog/announcing-evals-and-releases/); [Decagon](https://decagon.ai/blog/decagon-simulations)). No measured evidence was found that test-before-live features raise activation or trust; the support is vendor assertion and customer quotes. The security review is in effect a second funnel with its own drop-off (inference), and no source quantifies how often it blocks or how long it delays an install. **Confidence: moderate** that hand onboarding is right for the first cohort; **unknown** for the size of the security-review delay.

## The closest rivals show the product on your own app before any code ships

### Who is comparable, and who is left

The closest live product to Haptic is **Jimo's Adoption Agent**. Its documentation describes "a cursor and a tooltip on one control at a time; the user clicks", driven by reading the visible page's text and interactive elements ([Jimo docs](https://help.usejimo.com/docs/experiences/agent.md)). **Frigade** is closest in ambition: its assistant "highlights the next click and walks the workflow, across pages and modals", it can also act for the user, and it learns the app by sending its own browser agent in with a test login ([Frigade](https://frigade.com)). Product Fruits' assistant is adjacent, and a tail of small entrants (Skippr, Invocursor, and by snippet-only evidence Fluidworks and Orango) launched in 2025–2026. Several earlier entrants are gone or have moved. Y Combinator lists Layup, a "second cursor" guide, as Inactive ([YC](https://www.ycombinator.com/companies/layup)). Tandem's home page now sells "the agentic workspace for implementation and forward-deployed teams" and describes no embeddable script; no announcement of a pivot was found, so the evidence is the site content alone ([Tandem](https://usetandem.ai/)). Sammy Labs has repositioned, Command AI was absorbed into Amplitude after its October 2024 acquisition, and Fullstory bought Usetiful in November 2025 ([YC](https://www.ycombinator.com/companies/sammy-labs); [Amplitude](https://amplitude.com/blog/amplitude-acquires-command-ai); [Fullstory](https://www.fullstory.com/fullstory-acquires-usetiful/)).

Three readings of this field bear on Haptic; all three are the researchers' interpretation, not something any source states. First, the churn among pure "AI cursor" startups suggests the standalone category has been hard to sustain: the survivors bundled the pointer into a broader suite (Jimo, Product Fruits) or sell an interface to teams that already have an agent (Frigade). Second, the incumbent in-app tour platforms (Pendo, Appcues, Chameleon, Userpilot, Whatfix) have spent their AI effort on the authoring side, drafting tours and repairing broken selectors, not on pointing in answer to an end user's question. Third, most new entrants click for the user by default, so Haptic's "never clicks" rule is a minority position; Jimo's guide mode and Skippr's "guide-only mode" are the nearest equivalents. One fact needs no interpretation: **"one script tag" is the install claim of nearly every entrant** (Tandem's "One script to install", Invocursor's one tag, Skippr's "2 lines of code"), so it does not differentiate ([Product Hunt: Tandem](https://www.producthunt.com/posts/tandem-ai); [Product Hunt: Skippr](https://www.producthunt.com/posts/skippr-ai)). On commercial norms, Jimo and the mid-market tour tools are self-serve with 14-day to one-month trials and no card, at entry prices of roughly $80 to $300 a month (Jimo's Agent plan is $79 a month billed yearly); Frigade starts at $1,000 a month; WalkMe, Whatfix and Userlane are demo-only; and three leading AI support agents (Decagon, Sierra, Ada) offer no self-serve flow to study at all ([Jimo docs](https://help.usejimo.com/docs/settings/plan-and-billing.md); [Frigade pricing](https://frigade.com/pricing); [Decagon](https://decagon.ai)).

### What the best documented flows have in common

Three patterns recur among the self-serve leaders. The first is a first-run screen built from the customer's own site. Jimo asks for only a product URL and a goal at signup, then shows "the URL the Agent read", "how many pages it learned", drafts generated for the stated goal and a theme "picked from your site"; its "Get Started" page is a numbered checklist whose first step, "Workspace Created", is "already checked when you land" ([Jimo docs](https://help.usejimo.com/docs/getting-started/your-workspace.md)). Chatbase follows the Crisp shape in fewer screens: enter a URL, see "all discovered pages and the total character count", pass two skippable steps, test in a playground that "always uses the draft", then deploy ([Chatbase docs](https://www.chatbase.co/docs/user-guides/quick-start/your-first-agent)).

The second pattern is a trial on the customer's own logged-in app before any code. Jimo's Chrome extension "loads Jimo on any page you open in Chrome, as if the snippet were already installed", so a buyer can "build, position and preview experiences on your own app today. Only publishing waits for the snippet" ([Jimo docs](https://help.usejimo.com/docs/getting-started/extension)). Product Fruits, Userflow, Chameleon and UserGuiding document the same route ([Product Fruits](https://help.productfruits.com/article/test-in-chrome-without-installation); [Userflow](https://help.userflow.com/docs/userflow-chrome-extension); [Chameleon](https://help.chameleon.io/en/articles/1204283-what-does-the-chrome-extension-do); [UserGuiding](https://help.userguiding.com/en/articles/5358722-userguiding-now)). Jimo's documented order is workspace created, install the extension, create an experience, install the snippet, publish, and its docs note that only the install step needs a developer ([Jimo docs](https://help.usejimo.com/docs/getting-started/how-to-start.md)). Userpilot, Appcues, Pendo and Amplitude put the developer first; Appcues says plainly that it "only works on pages where the snippet loads" ([Appcues docs](https://docs.appcues.com/getting-started/quick-start-guide-for-appcues)). Frigade takes a third route: "Train on staging. Install the SDK in prod", after the customer sends "a URL and a test login", plus a public demo that needs no signup ([Frigade](https://frigade.com/how-it-works)).

The third pattern is a designed developer step. Jimo has a "Share Instructions to Engineer" button, tells the buyer to "Ask for a date", offers the install as two tabs (a prompt for an AI coding agent, or manual), shows the snippet "as detected on your domain", and extends the trial: "the trial lasts 2 weeks, plus 2 more weeks once the snippet is installed" ([Jimo install](https://help.usejimo.com/docs/getting-started/install-jimo.md); [Jimo installation settings](https://help.usejimo.com/docs/settings/installation.md); [Jimo billing](https://help.usejimo.com/docs/settings/plan-and-billing.md)). Userflow offers "Copy all instructions (to send to a developer)", writes a self-contained install specification file when its AI skill has no code access, and reports installation, initialisation and user identification as three separate checks ([Userflow install with AI](https://help.userflow.com/docs/install-with-ai); [Userflow installation check](https://help.userflow.com/docs/userflowjs-installation-check.md)). Plausible generates its own test traffic and, on failure, "lets you check again, try another URL, review the installation instructions or verify the installation manually" ([Plausible docs](https://plausible.io/docs/troubleshoot-integration)). Outside the guidance category, Intercom is the only flow found that covers an install-type question, framework-specific tabs, a "Test your installation" button, server-signed identity for logged-in users and a developer invite in one place ([Intercom help](https://www.intercom.com/help/en/articles/16946116-new-messenger-install-experience-on-your-website)).

The judgement on "who is best" is therefore split by job. Jimo has the most complete documented flow for a product like Haptic. Intercom has the most complete install for an app behind a login. Plausible has the best verification, PostHog the most mature automated install, and Chatbase the shortest value-first agent setup. **Confidence: moderate.** The ranking rests on documentation; real friction such as forced demo calls, email verification timing and work-email rules only shows up hands-on.

Times are this report's planning estimates; no source measures them. Use the same logged-in web app for every extension-based trial so the results compare, and use a throwaway demo app and scratch repository that Haptic controls wherever a vendor asks for a test login or code access.

## The cited paper credits the executor, not the map

### What arXiv:2510.04607 measured

The paper is "From Imperative to Declarative: Towards LLM-friendly OS Interfaces for Boosted Computer-Use Agents" by Wang, Li and Chen, published at EuroSys 2026 ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.04607)). It concerns agents that operate Windows desktop applications on their own. Its system, called the Declarative Model Interface, has two parts. Offline, an automated crawler clicks through Word, Excel and PowerPoint, records which control reveals which, and turns the result into a tree-like map with one unique path to each control; a pruned version of 15,000 to 30,000 tokens goes into the prompt. At run time, a new command lets the model name the control it wants, and a deterministic executor walks the path and clicks, with no further model calls for the hops in between: "Given a control identifier, DMI deterministically navigates from any current state to that control" ([arXiv full text](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.04607v2)). The evaluation used 27 single-application tasks, each run three times, with GPT-5 and GPT-5-mini.

| Configuration | GPT-5 success | GPT-5 model calls per task | GPT-5-mini success | GPT-5-mini model calls per task |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| No map (baseline) | 44.4% | 8.16 | 17.3% | 7.14 |
| Map in the prompt, no executor | 42.0% | 8.41 | 23.5% | 6.32 |
| Map plus executor | 74.1% | 4.61 | 43.2% | 4.43 |

The middle row is the test of the team's belief, and the authors ran it for exactly that reason: "To determine whether DMI's performance gains stem from its declarative interface or simply from providing a static knowledge base". Their conclusion for the strong model is that the map alone "yields no significant change in performance", "indicating that the declarative interface, not the static knowledge, is the primary source of DMI's effectiveness" ([arXiv full text](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.04607v2)). Three further points change how the paper should be read. First, the weaker model did gain modestly from the map alone, which the authors read as "supplementary topology knowledge can be helpful for models with less general-purpose knowledge". With 81 trials per configuration, that gain is 5 trials and the strong-model difference is 2; the paper reports no confidence intervals or significance tests (the trial arithmetic is the researchers' inference). Second, the "single LLM call" claim means four calls per task of which three are fixed framework overhead, and a successful task still took **239 seconds on average**. Per-call latency was "similar to the baseline", so the 39% time saving came entirely from making fewer calls, not faster ones. Third, the authors name Haptic's setting as the one they do not handle: "A canonical example is the Web browser. The main challenge of adapting DMI to Web browsers is the high volatility of Web controls". The offline model also cost under three hours of crawling plus "around 1.5 person-days" of manual work per application and is "version-specific" ([arXiv full text](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.04607v2)).

### Why the result does not carry over to Haptic as built

The reasoning runs in five steps. One: in the paper the map pays off because an executor clicks through the hidden hops at no model cost. In Haptic the executor is a person, by design, and the number of clicks is fixed by the app's own menus. The only savings available to Haptic are therefore model calls per human step and seconds per call. Two: Haptic makes one model call per step with a site map in the prompt, which is the middle row of the table, the configuration that showed no gain for a strong model. Three: Haptic's map is weaker than the paper's. It comes from a plain-HTTP crawl with no JavaScript and no login, and the web papers that build site graphs explicitly go beyond that: "Traditional crawlers parse static hyperlinks to discover URL-addressable pages but miss interaction-triggered states" ([WebNavigator](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.20366)). For a logged-in app, Haptic's repository says such pages "come back nearly empty", so the map holds little about the screens where users get lost. Four: the paper's "critical lesson is the need for applications to expose globally unique identifiers for controls", and Haptic's element identifiers are valid for one page snapshot only, so no stored route could be reused today. Five: Haptic logs no sessions or transitions, so nothing is learned after install either.

**The team's belief that a memory of the site will make Haptic "as fast as possible" is contradicted as stated.** What the paper supports is narrower: a map plus a mechanism that skips model calls reduces calls for an autonomous agent on desktop benchmark tasks. **Confidence: high** on what the paper says, which was read in full text with the numbers checked against its Table 3; **moderate** on the transfer to Haptic, because no study covers a web app, a logged-in app or a human in the loop.

### What the wider literature adds

The adjacent papers sort, imperfectly, by what the stored knowledge is used for. Knowledge the model only reads moves success by roughly 4 to 15 points and step counts by 0 to 25%. The large reductions all come from knowledge that is executed, although not every executable design achieves them (the grouping and the ranges are the researchers' synthesis).

| System and setting | What is stored | How it is used | Success | Model calls or steps | Wall-clock time | Caveat |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| DMI, Office desktop | Control-level navigation map | Model names target; executor navigates | 44.4% → 74.1% | 8.16 → 4.61 | 392 s → 239 s | 27 tasks; map alone gave nothing for the strong model |
| WebNavigator, WebArena benchmark sites | Graph of page states with click paths | Retrieve a page, model picks one, executor "teleports" | 50.4% → 63.3% | 10.10 → 8.98 | 773.6 s → 753.5 s | Tokens per task rose from 67,351 to 123,684 |
| ActionEngine, WebArena subset | State machine with validated locators | Model writes one program; it runs deterministically | 82.7% → 91.2% | 7–16 → 1.5–3.5 | 87 s → 27 s | Preprint; memory refined on the benchmark's own task templates; 73.1% with the crawler-only memory |
| MobileGPT, Android | Screens, sub-tasks and action sequences from first executions | Replay on repeat tasks | 82.7% new; 98.75% repeat | Not reported | Repeat tasks 62.5% faster; first run 3.9% slower | 16.2% of recalled actions still needed the model |
| WebChallenger, WebArena-lite | Per-site page summaries | Read by the model | 51.2% → 58.8% | 7.34 → 7.20 | Not reduced | Read-only: accuracy up, steps flat |
| Agent Workflow Memory, WebArena | Text workflows | Read by the model | 23.5% → 35.5% | 7.9 → 5.9 | Not reported | Top of the read-only range |

Sources for the table: ([DMI](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.04607v2); [WebNavigator](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.20366); [ActionEngine](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.20502); [MobileGPT](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.03003); [WebChallenger](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.10423); [Agent Workflow Memory](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.07429)).

Four findings from this body of work matter for Haptic. A graph does not guarantee time savings on the web: WebNavigator cut steps by 11–12% and left wall-clock time flat. The researchers' explanation is that finding the target, including an 11-second selection call, replaced the navigation steps it saved, and that most time is spent on the destination page ([WebNavigator](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.20366)). A raw map can hurt: adding an unsummarised map lowered one agent's success from 47.9% to 44.8%, and summarising it raised success to 57.4% ([WebATLAS](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.22732)). Speed on repeats is real but arrives on the second run: MobileGPT's 62.5% latency cut applies to a task type the system has already executed once ([MobileGPT](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.03003)). And nothing here was measured on a customer-specific logged-in application; one paper says so directly: "broader external validity (e.g., enterprise apps) remains to be tested" ([WALT](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.01524)).

The human-computer interaction evidence supports Haptic's product choice more than its speed claim. In a 20-person study on Google Sheets and Figma, a copilot that gave step-by-step visual guidance let 11 of 20 participants finish the Sheets task, against none with a copilot that did the task for them, and participants completed 88.5% of the task on average against 35.0% ([Khurana et al., CHI 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.15549)). In an earlier 16-person study, text-only help from a language model left users unable to find the controls it named, and they "often followed the LLM's suggestions verbatim, even if they were incorrect" ([Khurana et al., IUI 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.08030)). No controlled study was found that measures human task time or error rate for on-screen pointing against text instructions in a live web app.

### Where Haptic's speed can actually come from

The levers below are ordered by how little they ask of the customer. The first three ask nothing.

The largest untested lever is seconds per call. Haptic's default is a large model at low reasoning effort. Third-party measurements put time to first token for a non-reasoning small model (Claude Haiku 4.5) at **0.62 to 1.50 seconds** depending on the provider, and time to the first answer token for a newer small model at low reasoning effort at 6.28 seconds; the second figure is snippet-level and from a workload unlike Haptic's short prompts ([Artificial Analysis: Haiku 4.5](https://artificialanalysis.ai/models/claude-4-5-haiku/providers); [Artificial Analysis: Haiku 5.5 low](https://artificialanalysis.ai/models/claude-haiku-5-5-low/providers)). The researchers' inference is that Haptic is probably paying mostly for thinking time, not output. That must be measured on Haptic's own prompts, and no public benchmark tests small models on Haptic's actual task, choosing an element from a text description of the page. The risk is lost accuracy. Here the paper's ablation cuts the other way: the weaker model was the one that gained from the map. That yields the part of the team's belief that survives, as a hypothesis to test and not a finding: site knowledge will not make a strong model faster, but it could be what lets a faster, smaller model hold its accuracy.

The second lever is to move the pointer as soon as the target arrives. Haptic's structured output already puts `target` first; Anthropic documents streaming of tool input as it is generated, which allows the pointer to move on a partial result while the sentence follows (snippet-level) ([Claude docs](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/agents-and-tools/tool-use/fine-grained-tool-streaming)). The third is cache hygiene. Haptic's prompt is already cached, but the default cache lifetime is five minutes, so a low-traffic customer's first step is usually cold; a one-hour lifetime and pre-warming when the widget opens are documented options ([Claude docs](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-caching)). Anthropic's guidance for knowledge bases under about 200,000 tokens is to "just include the entire knowledge base in the prompt" with caching, and Haptic's 60,000-character help-docs budget sits well inside that, so no retrieval system is needed yet ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/news/contextual-retrieval)).

The fourth lever is the one the literature supports for large gains: skip the model on steps already seen. The design assembled from the papers and from browser-automation practice is a per-customer store of verified routes. Each entry maps a goal and a screen to a description of the element to point at, using role, visible name and position in the page structure, not a single selector. Before pointing, the entry is checked against the live page; a match points with no model call; a miss falls back to the normal call; and the correction is written back. The published precedent is Stagehand, whose cache returns "with no LLM inference and no token cost" and "falls back to full inference" when "the recorded selector no longer resolves"; its documentation puts cached replay at roughly 50–100 milliseconds against 1–5 seconds, a docs figure seen through a mirror, not a benchmark ([Stagehand docs](https://docs.stagehand.dev/v4/best-practices/caching); [Stagehand speed guide mirror](https://www.mintlify.com/browserbase/stagehand/best-practices/speed-optimization)). Matching on several attributes is the difference between a route that survives a redesign and one that does not: across new versions of 48 public sites, a single-locator baseline failed to find 27% of 801 elements and a multi-attribute matcher failed on 11% at about 4 milliseconds per lookup ([Similo](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1145/3571855)). Haptic has two structural advantages no autonomous agent has, both inferences: the person's click produces a settled page before the next pointer, which is a free check at every step, and every guided session is a demonstration of a real route with the user's goal attached. It also has a disadvantage: a wrong cached pointer is shown to a person, so the threshold for skipping the model must be conservative.

The fifth lever uses the user's reading time. A published system that has a fast model predict the next action reports up to 55% accuracy (snippet-level), which implies that roughly half of steps could be precomputed while the user reads and clicks ([Speculative Actions](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.04371)). The sixth is to use stable attributes such as test identifiers wherever a customer's app already has them. Two things are not viable as a primary path in 2026: Chrome's built-in model, which is desktop-only and needs at least 22 GB of free disk, and WebMCP, which is discussed below ([Chrome docs](https://developer.chrome.com/docs/ai/prompt-api)).

Levers four and five need routes, and routes have to come from somewhere: an admin walking the app, a recorded demonstration, or sessions after install. That is where the technical question becomes an onboarding question. The belief should be restated as something measurable: with a store of verified routes, what share of pointer steps can be served without a model call, at what pointer accuracy? The literature gives reason to expect a large share on repeat goals, since MobileGPT's memory hit rates on scripted repeats were 70.6–98.6%, and gives no number for help-widget traffic. **Confidence: moderate** on the direction of levers one and four; **low** on every size.

### "Just add the script tag" is the right production install and the wrong first step

The strongest case against the team's belief has four parts. First, the script tag is not a differentiator; nearly every entrant in the category makes the same claim. Second, it is rarely one step. Pendo, Fullstory, PostHog and Intercom all document Content Security Policy changes for their scripts, and buyers' security teams ask a standard list of privacy questions, including "Do you use customer data to train AI models?" ([Pendo CSP](https://support.pendo.io/hc/en-us/articles/360032209131); [PostHog CSP](https://posthog.com/docs/advanced/content-security-policy); [Vanta](https://www.vanta.com/resources/privacy-questions-enterprise-security-reviews)). In practice "one script tag" is one script tag, a policy change and a review, done by people who did not sign up (inference). Third, the only data on products where the buyer is not the installer says about half stall at this step for a month or more, and that figure is snippet-only. Fourth, the script by itself teaches Haptic nothing about the customer's app before users arrive. Haptic's website currently says "During onboarding we read your site and your help docs, so Haptic knows your product before it meets your users." For an app behind a login that sentence is true of the help docs and the public pages and false of the product's screens, because the intake crawl has no login and no JavaScript rendering. **That promise overstates what exists and should be reworded or made true.**

What still holds is substantial. A script tag is the only route to end users of a customer-facing app; an extension never reaches them. Haptic's install is also smaller than the incumbents'. Userflow, Appcues, Userpilot, Pendo and Amplitude all require a user-identification call as well as the snippet, and Jimo's docs budget "About an hour for the person who edits your app" for both; Haptic needs no identification call, stores no selectors and requires nothing to be authored ([Userflow docs](https://help.userflow.com/docs/userflowjs-installation.md); [Jimo docs](https://help.usejimo.com/docs/getting-started/install-jimo.md)). The site's other claim, that Haptic works behind a login because it "runs in the browser on the page your user is looking at", is accurate. Haptic can also verify faster than analytics tools: its first request from an allowed origin is proof of install within seconds, where Pendo warns that initial data "can take up to two hours" (the first half is an inference about Haptic's design; the second is documented) ([Pendo support](https://support.pendo.io/hc/en-us/articles/360046272771)).

The script tag therefore belongs after the buyer has seen Haptic work on their own app and before go-live, and it should land on a local or staging build first, then production for the customer's staff, then everyone. The exception is the signer who answers "yes, I can install it myself": for that person the install comes straight after signup, as it does at Sentry and PostHog. **Confidence: high** that the tag is table stakes and that five guidance vendors show the product before install; **moderate** that reordering will raise Haptic's install rate, which no source has tested for this kind of product.

### The Crisp/Hugo wizard transfers in shape, not in substance

The strongest counterargument to using Crisp as the model is that its wizard works for a reason Haptic does not share. Crisp's "Automatically train" step turns a public domain into a working support agent within minutes, and the agent's answers in the playground are the product. Every crawler in the evidence stops at a login: Intercom's documentation says "If the content you want to use is behind a login, Fin won't be able to access or import it", and Zendesk's says "If a website requires authentication, the web crawler can't access it" ([Intercom help](https://www.intercom.com/help/en/articles/9357945-sync-and-manage-websites); [Zendesk help](https://support.zendesk.com/hc/en-us/articles/9961827483162-Best-practices-for-using-a-web-crawler-to-import-content-for-AI-agents-AI-agents-Advanced-only)). Run on a Haptic customer, the same step would produce a bot that can answer from help articles, shown in a preview frame, which is a different product from a pointer on the customer's app. A second difference is the install. Crisp's widget goes on a public website, often through a platform plug-in the signer can add alone, and Crisp can leave it as one checklist item with manual verification. Haptic's goes into a logged-in application and needs an engineer. A third is the checklist: Crisp's ten items mix one critical step with nine general helpdesk tasks, which is why it opens at 9% (the researchers' reading).

| Crisp/Hugo step | Transfers? | Reasoning | Haptic version |
|---|---|---|---|
| Account by Google or name, email, password | Yes | One-click sign-in is the best-supported friction reducer | Same, with a nudge toward work email, not a block |
| Company, domain, goal chips | Partly | Haptic needs two addresses, the public site and the app where users log in, as Jimo records. Goal chips earn their place only if they change the flow | Company, public site, app URL, help-centre URL. Replace goal chips with the install question |
| AI-first or human-first choice | No | Haptic has no equivalent product split | The fork that matters: "Can you add a script to your app yourself?" |
| "Automatically train" on the domain | In shape only | Ask for one thing the customer knows, do work at once, show a countable result. The result for Haptic is intent knowledge from public pages, not the app | Show "N public pages and M help articles read" and say plainly that screens behind the login are learned in the next step |
| "Manually train" with website, files, Q&A | Yes | Maps onto Haptic's existing notes and docs inputs | Add the customer's five to ten most common how-do-I questions, which become the test set |
| "Configure" with logo, name, description, guidance, beside a live preview | Yes, with a different preview | A preview in an empty frame shows a chat bubble, not the pointer | Name, accent and guidance as now; the preview is the customer's own app |
| Dashboard opens on a playground: "disabled, a safe playground" | As a principle | "Disabled but testable" is the shared answer to fear of shipping an AI feature | Haptic's playground is the customer's app, visible to their staff only |
| "Setup required" badges | Yes | They show state, not a to-do | Script not detected; detected on staging; live for staff; live for everyone |
| "Get Started" at 9%, ten items, install second | No | Guidance is three to five items; about a fifth of users finish checklists; putting the engineer-dependent item second starts the list slowly | Five items, install fourth, first item pre-ticked with its reason |
| Activation as a separate switch | Yes | Install and go-live are different decisions | Staff-only, then everyone, with an off switch |

Crisp's flow also lacks five things Haptic needs because of where its script runs: the install question and developer hand-off, live install verification (Crisp's is "send a test message"), the list of allowed origins captured early as kapa.ai does before it reveals an ID, a security summary for the approver, and a staged rollout ([kapa.ai docs](https://docs.kapa.ai/integrations/website-widget/quickstart)). **Confidence: high** that the login wall breaks the "auto-train as proof" step, since vendor documentation and Haptic's own repository agree; **moderate** on the specific replacements, which are assembled from other vendors' documented patterns and untested for Haptic.

### How a prospect can see Haptic working on their own app

No route exists today, because the widget server is not deployed. The options, in the order the evidence and Haptic's current state favour them, are below.

| Route | What the prospect does | What it needs from Haptic | Limits | When to use it |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A call where their engineer adds the tag to a local or staging build | Joins a 30–45 minute call with one engineer | A deployed server; the site's allowed origins to include localhost and staging | Needs an engineer in the room | The first handful of customers |
| Chrome extension that injects the widget | Installs the extension, opens their app, asks a real question | Building and publishing an extension; its logic must ship inside the package | Company extension policies; Chrome only; a strict Content Security Policy can still block the server call; only that person sees it | Self-serve |
| Staging install from the hand-off page | Sends the page to a developer, who adds the tag to staging | The hand-off page and live verification | Still waits on the developer | Signers who answer "yes" or have a responsive engineer |
| Cloud browser the prospect logs into through a live view | Logs in once in a hosted browser; Haptic never holds the password | A hosted-browser setup | The researchers' suggestion, borrowed from browser-automation practice; not a documented pattern for product trials | Fallback where extensions are blocked |
| Public demo on a sample app | Nothing; no signup | A sample logged-in app with Haptic installed | Shows the product, not their app | Before signup |

The extension is the only approach found that shows a product on the prospect's real logged-in app with no credentials shared, no developer and no policy change, and it has a second payoff: the prospect's clicking during the trial can seed the routes Haptic lacks (inference). Its ceiling is IT policy; no data was found on what share of business admins may install an arbitrary extension. Pasting a loader into the browser console or using a bookmarklet is a weaker fallback that a strict policy will often block; that is an engineering assumption nobody sourced. **Confidence: moderate** that the extension is the right self-serve answer; **unknown** for how many of Haptic's prospects can use one.

## The install rate, the latency split and the legal line are all unmeasured

The gaps below are the ones the research could not close. They are grouped by what would close them.

**Numbers that do not exist publicly.** The install-completion rate for snippet products where the signer is not the installer. An activation benchmark for developer or infrastructure products. Any controlled test of an onboarding checklist against none. Any test of a blocking "waiting for first event" screen against a skippable one, or of demo data against none. Any measured effect of test-before-live features on activation or trust for AI agents. How often security review blocks or delays a client-side script, and which documents shorten it. Waitlist conversion and decay. Whether staged-rollout controls raise install rates. And the specific pattern this report recommends, a pre-install trial on the customer's own app for a product like Haptic, has been tested by no source; it is an extrapolation from Twilio, Chameleon, the guidance vendors' extensions and the AI-agent playgrounds.

**Competitor facts that need the hands-on pass.** Exact signup fields for every vendor; only Jimo's two are documented. Whether Frigade's "Get Started" is truly self-serve, how it stores test credentials, and whether training progress is visible. How Jimo's pointer behaves across full page loads and when the target is missing. Whether Intercom's and Chatbase's trials need a card or a sales call, whether Intercom's AI agent can be previewed before its Messenger is installed, and what Intercom's install test actually does. Whether any vendor shows a literal "waiting for your first event" screen. Whether Product Fruits' assistant draws walkthroughs live or launches pre-built tours. Why and when Tandem changed direction. What became of Command AI's copilot inside Amplitude. Intercom's tours with its AI agent, and Microsoft's and Google's screen-aware assistants, were not examined. Independent reviews on Reddit, Hacker News and G2 and the screen-recording libraries were not searched because the shared search budget ran out.

**Technical questions only Haptic's own tests can answer.** Whether a site map in the prompt helps a human-guided loop at all, and what it costs in time to first token with a cached prompt. Latency of the current large model at low effort on Haptic's short prompts. Accuracy of small models at choosing an element from page text. What share of real help questions repeat a goal already seen, which bounds the cache-hit rate. How fast stored routes go stale under a customer's release cadence. How often multi-attribute matching picks the wrong one of several near-identical controls, for example row actions in a table. How many logged-in screens each learning approach actually covers. No web paper in the notes crawled a multi-tenant app behind single sign-on, multi-factor login, role-based menus or feature flags. No study measures whether pointing is faster than text for people.

**Legal and security questions.** Whether a log of screen-to-screen transitions with no text, no values and no user identifier would be classed with session replay is a legal question the research cannot answer; the final French regulator text and the United States appeals opinion were not read. The payment-card standard's own text was not read at source. No source says when an early-stage vendor is expected to have a SOC 2 report. Whether a third-party script can list a page's WebMCP tools is not stated in Chrome's documentation. Claims about isolating the widget with shadow DOM were not sourced at all.

**Figures deliberately left out.** Several widely repeated statistics could not be traced to a primary source and are not used here: "40% to 60% of users who sign up will never return", product-qualified leads converting at 25–30%, 75% of users leaving in the first week, Stripe targeting a first API call in under 90 seconds, and a 40% conversion gain credited to Intercom's onboarding rethink. Userpilot states its benchmark sample as both 62 and 547 companies. The headline figures from the two loyalty-card psychology studies come from secondary summaries, not the papers. Results from classic tutorial research in human-computer interaction were verified bibliographically only and should not be quoted with numbers.

## Recommended flow: proof, then hand-off, then staff, then everyone

### The ordered flow

This is a design recommendation. It describes what the customer experiences, whether Haptic's team performs a step by hand or a dashboard does.

| # | Step | What happens | Who at the customer | Reason |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | Before signup | A public demo of the pointer on a sample app, no signup | Anyone | Onboarding starts before signup; Frigade and Chameleon offer the same. The Request access form is already part of onboarding |
| 1 | Signup | Work email, company, public site, app URL where users log in, help-centre URL, and one question: "Can you add a script to your app yourself?" (yes; someone else will; not sure) | Buyer | Ask only what forks the flow. The install question is the single one the evidence justifies, and the answer splits every later metric |
| 2 | Read what is public and say so honestly | Crawl the public site and help docs. Show "N public pages and M help articles read", pick name and accent from the site, and state that screens behind the login are learned next | Buyer | Jimo's "What Jimo knows" screen; something real within minutes. Honest about the login wall, which the current website copy is not |
| 3 | See it on your own app | First cohort: on a call, their engineer adds the tag to a local or staging build. Self-serve: the buyer installs an extension and opens their app | Buyer; an engineer only in the first-cohort version | The aha is "it works on my app". Five guidance vendors put this before the install. Sentry's sample-event test is the one controlled result that showing success first raises installs |
| 4 | Teach and test the top questions | The buyer asks their five to ten most common how-do-I questions on their own app, marks each pointer right or wrong, and demonstrates the route where it was wrong. The questions are saved as a test set and the routes as verified routes | Buyer or support lead | This is the trust step AI-agent vendors use, and the only source of knowledge about logged-in screens before launch. Demonstrations gave the best results in the one comparison found. It is also what later makes repeat steps fast |
| 5 | Hand-off to the installer | One page: the script tag with the site ID filled in, the origins to allow, the two Content Security Policy lines, a prompt for an AI coding agent, and a one-page summary of what the script reads, masks and sends. "I'll do it" opens the page; "Send to my developer" emails it with Haptic copied in. Ask for a date | Buyer sends; engineer acts; security reads | The largest expected drop is between "buyer convinced" and "script detected". Upscope, Jimo, Userflow and Intercom all design this step; coding-agent prompts are the fastest-growing install path |
| 6 | Verify | The status changes when the first request arrives from an allowed origin and names the environment. It separates "script not found", "origin not allowed" and "blocked by policy". The waiting screen shows what success looks like and is never a dead end | Engineer | A vendor cannot load a logged-in page, so a first-request listener is the mechanism that transfers. Plausible pairs each failure with a next action |
| 7 | Go live in stages | Staging, then production for the customer's own staff, then all users, with an off switch | Buyer decides; engineer may gate | Intercom documents internal-first rollout; AI-agent vendors add limited release. It lets a cautious customer say yes in parts |
| 8 | Activation and after | The first end-user question answered in production is the account's activation. A weekly summary shows questions asked, wrong pointers and goals with no answer. Learning routes from guided sessions is opt-in | Buyer | Setup is not the aha. The summary is the reason to come back, and the opt-in keeps the privacy question explicit |

For a signer who answers "yes" to the install question, steps 5 and 6 come directly after step 2 and steps 3 and 4 happen on their own local build.

The team has put the dashboard and its checklist off until later, and the evidence supports that order: the checklist is the part of the Crisp flow with the weakest support. When the dashboard is built, its "Get Started" list should hold five items worded as goals, shown as "1 of 5" and not as a percentage that opens at 9%.

| Item | Done when | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Workspace created: Haptic read your public site and help docs | On arrival; pre-ticked | A head start with a stated reason, the condition under which the effect held |
| See Haptic guide you on your own app | The widget has answered one question on the app's domain | Completable without an engineer |
| Check your top five questions | Five questions asked and each marked right or corrected | Completable without an engineer; creates the test set |
| Add the script to your app | A request is seen from an allowed origin | Two buttons, "I'll do it" and "Send to my developer"; shows sent, opened, detected on staging, detected on production |
| Turn it on for your team, then for everyone | Live for all users | The go-live switch |

Branding, team invites, billing and integrations stay in settings. A generic "invite your team" item drew about 1% use at Rows. Checklist completion must not be the activation metric.

### Staged path

| | First handful of customers, by hand | Self-serve, built afterwards |
|---|---|---|
| Entry | Keep "Request access". Add the app URL, the help-centre URL and the install question to its second step. Someone on the team replies personally | Open signup with Google or work email; keep a human lane for larger accounts |
| Learning the public site | The team runs the existing intake command on the site, notes and docs, and reviews the pages it flags as thin | The same crawl behind a "what Haptic knows" screen |
| Seeing it on their app | A call with the buyer and one engineer; the tag goes onto a local or staging build during the call | Extension; hand-off page for staging |
| Teaching the top questions | The team watches the buyer ask their real questions, writes owner notes for each wrong pointer, and keeps the list as that account's test set | In-product "mark right or wrong, then show me", saved as verified routes |
| Install and verification | The team sends the tag and policy lines by email, stays on the thread, and confirms by hand that requests arrive | Hand-off page, coding-agent prompt, live status |
| Go-live | Agreed on the call: staging, staff, everyone | Rollout controls and off switch |
| Record-keeping | One sheet per account with a timestamp for each stage, every confusion, and every security question asked | Instrumented account funnel |
| Prerequisites | A deployed widget server; a record of when each site's script was first seen and of each guide's steps; a one-page summary of what the script reads, masks and sends | Accounts, a database, a dashboard |

The hand stage is forced as much as chosen: Haptic has no accounts, no dashboard and no deployed server, and the parts of the flow most likely to fail, the hand-off and the security review, are the parts analytics cannot explain. The notes from those calls are the specification for self-serve. The criteria for opening self-serve recur across Arcade and Superhuman and none is a number: inbound exceeds what can be handled by hand, the team can write down the confusions the calls were resolving, and the product no longer needs a person to cover a missing function. The self-serve build order should follow where the first cohort stalls. On present evidence that is the hand-off page with verification first, then the extension. **Confidence: moderate.**

## Conclusion

Onboarding and speed turned out to be one design problem, not two. The only kind of memory the evidence credits with large gains is a store of executable, verified routes, and for an app behind a login those routes can only come from people: an admin showing Haptic where things are before launch, and end users being guided after it. The step in the flow where a buyer tests their top questions and corrects the pointer is therefore not a courtesy before the "real" install. It is where Haptic acquires the knowledge its website already claims to have, where the buyer decides whether to trust it, and where the material for model-free steps is first collected. A flow modelled on Crisp would skip that step, because Crisp can get everything it needs from a public URL.

The research also changes what Haptic can claim. "A memory of the site makes it fast" should become a hypothesis with two numbers attached, the share of steps served without a model call and the pointer accuracy at that share, and "one script tag" should be treated as the price of entry, with the difference made by what happens before and after it. Haptic's real structural advantages are untested ones: a person's click verifies every step for free, and every guided session is a labelled demonstration in the real app with real permissions. No published system has measured either, which means the first five customers will produce evidence that does not exist anywhere else, provided each stage of their journey is written down.
