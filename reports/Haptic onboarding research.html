Research report · revised 12 November 2026

Haptic must earn its script tag

  • 9 sections
  • About 74 minutes in full
  • Sections 1 and 2 answer the team's review: about 17 minutes

Bottom line. Three assumptions the team started with each fail in the form stated, and correcting them changes the order of Haptic's onboarding more than its contents. The fix has three parts: show Haptic working on the customer's own site, before the install or straight after a small one; treat the hand-off to whoever installs the script as a designed step with live verification; and get speed from routes that people have confirmed, not from a site map in the prompt.

It is the first step that needs an engineer, and often a Content Security Policy change and a security review as well. One comparable snippet product reported almost half its signups still uninstalled after a month (seen only in a search snippet). On a public site, "enter your domain" can do for Haptic what it does for Crisp once the crawl can render JavaScript, which Haptic's cannot yet. On an app behind a login, no crawler in the evidence can read the screens, Haptic's own included, so the proof has to come another way High on the login wall; moderate on the replacements

What follows. Keep the request-access gate and onboard the first handful of companies by hand. Build only the three steps every script-installed product needs (create a site, add the tag, see it connect) and add the others when a named problem calls for them. Test the choice of model before anything else, because it asks nothing of the customer. The number that matters most, the share of signups who are not engineers and still get a script installed, is published nowhere, so the first customers' own funnel is the most valuable measurement available.

01 13 min read

Team review, 12 November: decisions and open questions

Everything in this section is this report's judgement, reasoned from the evidence in the later sections and from Haptic's code. None of it has been measured on Haptic.

Speed first, or accuracy first

The team's stated goal is to be as fast as possible, and then as accurate as possible. The strongest argument against that order is that a wrong pointer is not a slow answer. It is a wrong instruction given with authority. In the one study in this report of people following model help, users "often followed the LLM's suggestions verbatim, even if they were incorrect" (Khurana et al., IUI 2024). A fast wrong pointer costs the user the wrong click, the time to notice, the way back, and their trust in the next pointer. A right pointer that arrives a second later costs one second. Haptic's pitch against chatbots is that it shows exactly where to click, so accuracy is the product and speed is how good the product feels.

What holds in the team's order is that above some level of accuracy, speed is what people notice, and Haptic today is probably slow. The long-standing rule of thumb for response times is that about 0.1 seconds feels instant, about 1 second keeps a person's train of thought, and about 10 seconds is the limit of attention (Nielsen, 1993; a design guideline, not a measurement of Haptic, and not part of the research notes). The pointer should aim for the middle figure.

The rule this report recommends: set an accuracy floor, then make Haptic as fast as possible without going under it. In practice that means a fixed set of real questions, a pass mark the team chooses for "the first pointer was right", and the fastest configuration that clears it. The research gives no number for the pass mark. The two goals also stop competing on repeat questions: a route a person has confirmed is both faster than a fresh model answer, because there is no model call, and more accurate, because someone checked it. Confidence: moderate.

Which model

The model in use is probably not the right one for speed, and nobody has measured it. Three facts, from Haptic's code and from Anthropic's model reference as bundled with this session (cached 3 November 2026; check the live pricing page before budgeting):

Recommendation: decide by test, not by reputation. Run the three on the same questions, in the same hour, and compare two numbers: seconds until the pointer moves, and the share of first pointers that are right. This report's expectation, unmeasured, is that Haiku 5.5 with thinking off and the reply streamed will reach the first pointer several times sooner, and that the open question is whether it stays accurate on real apps. The cited paper gives one reason for hope: its weaker model was the one that gained from a site map. If the small model fails the accuracy floor only on hard steps, it can take every step and hand just the failures (no target found, or the loop guard in src/widget/guide.ts tripping) to a larger model. A faster model is not automatically a faster guide: one that needs more steps gives the time back, so compare whole guides as well as single steps.

Two changes are worth making whichever model wins, and neither asks anything of the customer. Stream the reply and move the pointer as soon as target arrives. And give the cached site prompt a one-hour lifetime: the default is five minutes, so on a quiet site the first step of most guides starts cold. Pre-warming with an empty request is not allowed together with structured output, which Haptic uses, so the longer lifetime is the available fix.

This session had no API key, so nothing was timed. About 30 questions on 3 models is a few hundred calls, likely under ten dollars at the prices above; that figure is an estimate from token counts, not a quote. Confidence: moderate that the current model is the wrong default for speed; unknown for which model holds accuracy.

Showing the steps of a guide

Jimo's marketing demo shows a panel that lists the steps of a guide and ticks them off: "Read page: Home", "Find Reports in the sidebar", "Plan the export in 3 steps", "Open Reports", then the filter and the export. Frigade's tooltip shows "3 of 3". These are screenshots from the two companies' sites, not observed product behaviour. A step list is not required. Part of it is worth having, and the part that is free should come first.

What the panel shows What it costs Haptic Recommendation
Steps already done Nothing. The guide already keeps this history in src/widget/guide.ts Show it now. It tells the user where they are and that progress is real
The current step Nothing. It is the sentence beside the pointer Already shown
Steps still to come A plan made before the first pointer moves: an extra model call or a longer first reply, which works against speed. The plan is also a guess, because Haptic sees only the page the user is on Show only when the steps come from a verified route, where the whole list is known, costs nothing, and has been checked
"Reading the page", "Planning" Nothing, but these are not steps the user takes. They narrate the wait Use one short status line, as Haptic does now ("Finding it…"). If the wait needs three lines to fill, the wait is the problem

That makes the step list the visible face of the route store. On a goal Haptic has seen, the user gets "3 steps" at once and the pointer moves immediately. On a new goal, they get one step at a time. Jimo's demo also corrects a wrong click ("AND, not OR"). Haptic already detects a click on something other than the target and carries on from where the user is (waitForAction in src/widget/guide.ts); saying so in the pointer's sentence would be a small change. Confidence: moderate.

Which onboarding steps are required

The seven-step flow recommended at the end of this report is not proven to be the best option, and as something to build now it is too much. The report's own confidence in the order is moderate at best. Three of the seven steps are required by any product installed with a script: create a site, add the tag, see it connect. Every snippet product in the research has those three. The other four each answer a named problem, and each can be dropped when the problem does not apply.

Step Required? The problem it answers Drop or defer it when
1. Sign up Yes Haptic needs an owner, a site ID and the origins the widget may run on Never. The question "can you install it yourself?" need not be a signup field: two buttons on the install page, "I'll add it" and "Send to my developer", collect the same answer
2. Read the public pages and help docs No Gives the customer something specific to them within minutes, and gives Haptic their vocabulary The crawl returns little. It must never block the next step
3. See it on your own site before the install No The person who signed up cannot install it and needs a reason to chase someone who can The signer can install it in minutes. That is likely for the first customers, and then the install is the demo
4. Test your top questions No, but it is the first one to keep Trust before real users see it; a test set; the first verified routes It should not be dropped. It can run after the install, in a preview only the admin sees
5. Add the script Yes The script has to reach the site Never. For a signer who installs alone it is one tag and a copy button
6. See it connect Yes Without it nobody knows whether the install worked, and every support conversation starts with guessing Never
7. Go live in stages No Lets a cautious customer say yes in parts Public sites and small teams. One switch, off by default, is enough

The lean version is five screens, and the last two are the product itself and not setup: sign up with the site's address; copy the tag or send it to a developer; watch the status change to connected; try the top questions in a preview only the admin can see; switch it on. The preview does the work of steps 3, 4 and 7 with one mechanism and no browser extension: the tag is installed, but the widget appears only in a browser the admin opened from a preview link in the dashboard. Google Tag Manager's preview mode works the same way. The cost is that the proof now comes after the install, so it does nothing for a signer who cannot get the tag installed. For that person the call with an engineer, or later the extension, is still the answer, and the first customers' funnel will show how common that person is.

What this report still holds after the challenge: the install is where accounts stall, verification is not optional, the top-questions step is where both trust and routes come from, and staged release matters for apps that hold customer data. What it concedes: seven separate steps is over-built, the install question does not belong on the signup form, and the extension should not be built until the first customers show that signers cannot install. Confidence: moderate.

Is a Supabase project needed

Not for this week's goal. The server keeps each site as a JSON file, which works as long as it runs on one machine with a disk. A database becomes necessary at three points.

When Why files stop working
The server runs somewhere with no disk, or on more than one machine Cloudflare Workers, where the website already runs, has no disk. Two machines would each have their own copy of the files
The step log is being written Many requests appending to one file at once is fragile, and the log is only useful if it can be queried
There are accounts Sign-in, and rules about who can see which site, need a real store

Supabase covers all three: a Postgres database, sign-in with Google or email, and per-row access rules. The change on Haptic's side is small, because every read and write of a site goes through three functions in src/server/sites.ts. Recommendation: one machine with a disk this week; create the Supabase project when the step log or signup is started, whichever comes first.

Four things to know before then. The Supabase account connected to this session has no project for Haptic, and a team project belongs in an organisation the whole team can reach. The server must hold the database's service key as a secret and never send it to the browser. Site contexts should stay cached in the server's memory, so that a step never waits on a database read. And free Supabase projects are paused after about a week without activity, as far as this report knows; if the widget depended on a paused project it would stop on every customer's site, so check the current terms before production relies on a free project. If the server moves to Cloudflare Workers, Cloudflare's own storage is the alternative for site contexts (the website already uses its key-value store for access requests), but it has no sign-in service. Confidence: high that it is not needed this week; moderate on the timing after that.

Note for Isaac: a store of verified routes

What it is. For each site, a small table that remembers which control was right for a goal on a screen. When the same goal comes up again on the same screen, Haptic points without asking the model, after checking that the control is on the live page. If the check fails, it falls back to the model call that exists today, and the new answer replaces the stored one.

Why build it. It is the only kind of memory in the research with large measured cuts in model calls. The cited paper went from 8.16 to 4.61 calls per task with an executor, and to nothing with the map alone. An Android system made repeat tasks 62.5% faster after a first run (MobileGPT). A browser-automation library documents cached replay at roughly 50 to 100 milliseconds against 1 to 5 seconds for a model call (Stagehand docs; a documentation figure, not a benchmark). The site map Haptic puts in the prompt today is the kind of memory that showed no gain for a strong model.

What the repo already has.

Everything a route step needs The guide loop in src/widget/guide.ts: the goal, the page path, the element pointed at, whether the user clicked it (last.used), and what changed afterwards All of it is in memory during a guide and none of it is saved
One place every step passes through POST /v1/turn in src/server/index.ts, which calls runTurn in src/server/model.ts The store can sit in front of runTurn with no change to the widget
Per-site storage JSON files in data/sites/, read by src/server/sites.ts A first version can keep routes the same way

The smallest version worth building, in order.

  1. Log before acting. On every step, record the goal, the normalised path, any open dialog, the key of the element pointed at, whether the user clicked it, and whether the guide finished. This alone answers the question that decides everything else: what share of real questions repeat a goal already seen on the same screen.
  2. Serve exact repeats. Look up by site, goal (lower-cased, punctuation removed), path, dialog and the key of the previous step. Store the element key plus two tie-breakers: the context string the snapshot already computes (such as "nav › Account") and the position among controls with the same key. If an entry exists and exactly one element in the live snapshot matches, return it with the stored sentence and skip the model.
  3. Check and repair. If nothing matches, or more than one element does, call the model as now and overwrite the entry. Count hits, misses and repairs.
  4. Serve only what a person confirmed. An entry becomes servable once a user clicked the element it points at and the guide went on to finish, or once an admin confirmed it during onboarding.

Company onboarding ends at the first real end-user answer, not at the install

The account is the unit, and setup is not the "aha"

There is no single authoritative definition of onboarding. The practitioners cited most often agree on three points. First, onboarding starts before signup and has no fixed end: Samuel Hulick defines it as "any time there's an opportunity to increase the likelihood that users are successful" and places the start "long before they sign up" (Intercom interview). Second, the measurable milestone inside it is activation, "the earliest point in your onboarding flow that, by showing your product's value, is predictive of long-term retention" (Lenny's Newsletter). Third, in B2B the thing that must activate is the account, not the person who signed up: Miro did not count an account as activated until two people had collaborated, and PostHog checks whether "an organization meets the activation definition within 30 days" (Elena Verna; PostHog handbook). Verna, quoting the Reforge definition, splits activation into setup, aha and habit and names the common error directly: "Many companies excel at innovating in setup and mistakenly want to count setup as their value delivery, or aha moment" (Elena Verna).

Applied to Haptic, a company onboarding flow is the whole account-level path from the "Request access" form on joinhaptic.com to the point where the customer's own end users are being guided and coming back. Installing the script is setup. The aha is the buyer watching the pointer find the right control on their own app. Activation is the first real end-user question answered in production, and habit is repeat use in the following weeks. The person who signs up for Haptic, the person who installs it and the person who approves it are often three different people, so "the user activated" and "the account activated" are different events (an inference from the definitions above). For orientation only: self-reported median activation across surveys sits between about 25% and 37% of signups, each source using its own milestone, and products sold to developers convert free users to paid at about half the rate of other software (5% against 10% within six months in a secondary analysis of OpenView's 2023 survey) (Lenny's Newsletter; Userpilot; boldstart). With a handful of accounts these are not targets; the funnel should be read as a list of named companies. Confidence: high that this is the consensus definition. It is framework and opinion, not measurement.

No one publishes how many signups finish a snippet install

The planning number Haptic needs most is the share of signups that complete a snippet or SDK install when the person signing up is not the person installing. The researchers searched for it across Segment, Hotjar, Mixpanel, Amplitude, Heap, FullStory, LaunchDarkly, Stripe, Algolia and Intercom and found only help pages. Two weak anchors exist. Upscope, a co-browsing product installed by snippet, wrote that "almost half did not have Upscope installed after one month" (snippet only; the post could not be opened) (Upscope). Sentry, whose signups are developers, reported that about 70% of users on its two best platforms sent a first event within two days in 2017 (Sentry). Sentry is a ceiling case, because its signups can install the product themselves. From these two points, expecting well under half of non-developer signups to reach a live install without human help is a reasonable planning assumption. It is an estimate from two weak data points, not a benchmark.

Three levers are reported to raise the install rate, in rising order of claimed effect. The first is making instructions specific to the customer's stack, which Sentry did by asking for the platform at signup (Sentry). The second is routing the task to the right person. Upscope asked each signup whether they could install the product themselves, showed the code to those who said yes, and gave everyone else a one-click email to their developer with Upscope copied in; it claimed this halved the share still uninstalled after a month (snippet only, no sample size, no control) (Upscope). Intercom documents the same idea as a developer invite carrying "the minimum permissions required to install the Messenger" (Intercom help). The third is removing the manual work. PostHog reports that users of its command-line install wizard convert to paid at 14.2% against 2.6% and reach a first event in 1.9 hours against 3.8, and DevCycle reports about three times as many users reaching SDK install after moving the install into the developer's AI coding assistant (PostHog; DevCycle). Both are self-reported and uncontrolled. PostHog's post names no sample, period or assignment method, and developers who choose a wizard are likely more committed than those who do not, so the gap overstates the causal effect. Amplitude is running a 50/50 test of a coding-agent install option and has published no result (Amplitude docs). Confidence: moderate on direction, because independent companies report the same thing; low on every magnitude.

Value before the hard step helps; blocking helps only when the step is the value

The direction of the evidence favours letting the buyer experience something real before the install, and nearly all of it is self-reported. Chameleon, itself a snippet-installed guidance product, built a sandbox that needs no code and reported that users who explored it "reached key milestones 25% faster"; those users chose to explore, so motivation is a confound (Chameleon). Twilio's redesign, which included a simulated phone that receives test messages before any carrier registration, reported "a 62% improvement in first message activation" with no baseline or sample given (Twilio). The one controlled result is Sentry's: a prominent "See Sample Event" button at the top of the install-instructions page beat control by 12% on the share of signups that went on to send a real error (Sentry). Showing what success looks like on the waiting screen raised real installs. Every vendor in the notes that kept the install first has also added a way out of the waiting screen: Sentry's sample error, PostHog's skip action, Microsoft Clarity's public demo project. No fetched source shows a hard block with no alternative (Sentry source; PostHog source; Microsoft Learn).

There is evidence in the other direction, and it concerns a different question. Superhuman made one setup step full-screen and interruptive and saw completion go "from 30% to over 98%"; when its onboarding call went from mandatory to optional, attendance fell from 100% to a typical 15% (First Round Review). The two findings fit together once the questions are separated. Should the buyer see value before the hard step? Mostly yes. Should a step be blocking? Only when the step itself is the value and the person in front of it can perform it. A blocking "waiting for your script" screen fails the second test whenever the signer is not an engineer (inference). For Haptic, generic demo data is a weak substitute for the real thing, because the aha is "it works on my app"; no source tested a pre-install trial on the customer's own app for a product of this type. Confidence: moderate.

Ask only what changes the next screen

Removing signup fields raises signups, but the better-supported finding is that questions which route the user cost little. One-click sign-in through a third-party account was associated with signup conversion 8.2 percentage points higher across 79 companies (2017, observational) (Heap). Rows nudged users toward a company email and saw a "33% increase in the share of users registering with a work email" with website conversion unchanged at 5–6%; its use-case question, which changed nothing downstream, drew "Not sure" or "Other" from 25% of respondents; and its generic invite-teammates step was used by about 1% of new signups (pre/post comparison, sample sizes not reported) (Rows). Twilio's role and code-or-no-code question forks the flow and was part of its reported activation gain, though its separate contribution was not isolated (Twilio). The lesson is to ask only what the product will branch on. For Haptic that is one question: can you add a script to your app yourself? Invites work the same way. In a controlled 30-day test, Sentry let any member request an invite for a colleague and let outsiders ask to join, which together raised accepted invitations by 21%, and 71–78% of invitees became active; Rows's generic invite step was used by about 1% (Sentry). The invite that matters for Haptic is "send this to the person who can install it". Confidence: moderate.

Checklists reach about a fifth of users, and the psychology cited for them is thin

In the one vendor dataset that reports it, the average onboarding checklist completion rate is 19.2%, and in another about 23% of users click inside a checklist launcher at all (Userpilot; Chameleon). No public controlled test of checklist against no checklist, with activation as the outcome, was found. The psychology usually cited is weaker than vendor posts suggest. The endowed-progress study is real and peer-reviewed, but it concerned loyalty cards redeemed for a tangible reward, and its abstract says the effect depends on "the reason, if any, offered for the endowment" (Journal of Consumer Research). A 2025 meta-analysis "found no memory advantage for unfinished tasks", so the Zeigarnik effect should not be cited for checklists (Ghibellini and Meier). A meta-analysis of 32 randomised web-survey experiments found that a constant progress indicator "does not significantly help reduce drop-offs" and that indicators which start slowly increase drop-off (Villar, Callegaro and Yang). Sentry found in 2019 that its onboarding widget, "a list of TODOs rather than a guided tour of the product", had low engagement and replaced it with contextual help that raised its first-event rate by almost 6% (Sentry). Vendor guidance is three to five items, with no data behind it (ProductLed).

Three design consequences follow, all inferences. A checklist is a secondary aid for the motivated minority, so the main path has to be the flow itself. A pre-ticked first item is legitimate when it has a stated reason, which is the condition under which the head-start effect held. And a list whose first open item is "wait for an engineer" starts slowly, which is the pattern that raised drop-off in surveys; items the buyer can finish alone belong first. The transfer from surveys to onboarding is the researchers' reasoning, not a tested result. Confidence: moderate that a checklist is secondary; low on the right number of items.

Human help, staged exposure and the security review belong inside the flow

The evidence on gating is founder testimony, and it is consistent. Superhuman's head of onboarding says "every founder should spend time manually onboarding their earliest customers" and reports human-led onboarding at "nearly 2x" the activation of self-serve, while noting selection bias; replacing it in-product took three years (First Round Review). Arcade ran a waitlist until it "got to know our first one hundred customers very well", learned that users "were confused about the first action that they needed to take", and opened self-serve when it could no longer keep up with leads (Arcade). Paul Graham's name for doing the install with the customer is the "Collison installation", after Stripe's founders: "Right then, give me your laptop" (Paul Graham). Survey data point the same way and are correlational: companies that reached out to users "retained them 2-3x better", and 80% of free-trial products add human touchpoints when an enterprise user enters the trial (OpenView via PR Newswire; ChartMogul). No benchmark for waitlist conversion or decay was found.

A script inside a customer's app runs with that app's privileges, so a reviewer treats it as code the customer is shipping. The documentation of mature vendors shows what reviewers expect: exact Content Security Policy entries (the browser rule list naming which hosts a page may load scripts from and send data to), a pinnable version, masking done in the browser before anything leaves it, and a rollout that starts with internal users (PostHog docs; FullStory help; Intercom help). Payment pages carry an extra burden: PCI DSS requirement 6.4.3 says every script on a payment page must be authorised, integrity-checked and listed in a written inventory with a justification, which matters to any guide that points at billing screens (wording from a practitioner summary, not the standard itself) (Report URI). AI support agents have converged on a similar caution: train on the customer's content, test in a playground, test in bulk against real past questions, then release to a limited audience, and Intercom and Decagon both shipped formal evaluation and release tooling in 2025–2026 (Intercom; Decagon). No measured evidence was found that test-before-live features raise activation or trust; the support is vendor assertion and customer quotes. The security review is in effect a second funnel with its own drop-off (inference), and no source quantifies how often it blocks or how long it delays an install. Confidence: moderate that hand onboarding is right for the first cohort; unknown for the size of the security-review delay.

The closest rivals show the product on your own app before any code ships

Who is comparable, and who is left

The closest live product to Haptic is Jimo's Adoption Agent. Its documentation describes "a cursor and a tooltip on one control at a time; the user clicks", driven by reading the visible page's text and interactive elements (Jimo docs). Frigade is closest in ambition: its assistant "highlights the next click and walks the workflow, across pages and modals", it can also act for the user, and it learns the app by sending its own browser agent in with a test login (Frigade). Product Fruits' assistant is adjacent, and a tail of small entrants (Skippr, Invocursor, and by snippet-only evidence Fluidworks and Orango) launched in 2025–2026. Several earlier entrants are gone or have moved. Y Combinator lists Layup, a "second cursor" guide, as Inactive (YC). Tandem's home page now sells "the agentic workspace for implementation and forward-deployed teams" and describes no embeddable script; no announcement of a pivot was found, so the evidence is the site content alone (Tandem). Sammy Labs has repositioned, Command AI was absorbed into Amplitude after its October 2024 acquisition, and Fullstory bought Usetiful in November 2025 (YC; Amplitude; Fullstory).

Three readings of this field bear on Haptic; all three are the researchers' interpretation, not something any source states. First, the churn among pure "AI cursor" startups suggests the standalone category has been hard to sustain: the survivors bundled the pointer into a broader suite (Jimo, Product Fruits) or sell an interface to teams that already have an agent (Frigade). Second, the incumbent in-app tour platforms (Pendo, Appcues, Chameleon, Userpilot, Whatfix) have spent their AI effort on the authoring side, drafting tours and repairing broken selectors, not on pointing in answer to an end user's question. Third, most new entrants click for the user by default, so Haptic's "never clicks" rule is a minority position; Jimo's guide mode and Skippr's "guide-only mode" are the nearest equivalents. One fact needs no interpretation: "one script tag" is the install claim of nearly every entrant (Tandem's "One script to install", Invocursor's one tag, Skippr's "2 lines of code"), so it does not differentiate (Product Hunt: Tandem; Product Hunt: Skippr). On commercial norms, Jimo and the mid-market tour tools are self-serve with 14-day to one-month trials and no card, at entry prices of roughly $80 to $300 a month (Jimo's Agent plan is $79 a month billed yearly); Frigade starts at $1,000 a month; WalkMe, Whatfix and Userlane are demo-only; and three leading AI support agents (Decagon, Sierra, Ada) offer no self-serve flow to study at all (Jimo docs; Frigade pricing; Decagon).

What the best documented flows have in common

Three patterns recur among the self-serve leaders. The first is a first-run screen built from the customer's own site. Jimo asks for only a product URL and a goal at signup, then shows "the URL the Agent read", "how many pages it learned", drafts generated for the stated goal and a theme "picked from your site"; its "Get Started" page is a numbered checklist whose first step, "Workspace Created", is "already checked when you land" (Jimo docs). Chatbase follows the Crisp shape in fewer screens: enter a URL, see "all discovered pages and the total character count", pass two skippable steps, test in a playground that "always uses the draft", then deploy (Chatbase docs).

The second pattern is a trial on the customer's own logged-in app before any code. Jimo's Chrome extension "loads Jimo on any page you open in Chrome, as if the snippet were already installed", so a buyer can "build, position and preview experiences on your own app today. Only publishing waits for the snippet" (Jimo docs). Product Fruits, Userflow, Chameleon and UserGuiding document the same route (Product Fruits; Userflow; Chameleon; UserGuiding). Jimo's documented order is workspace created, install the extension, create an experience, install the snippet, publish, and its docs note that only the install step needs a developer (Jimo docs). Userpilot, Appcues, Pendo and Amplitude put the developer first; Appcues says plainly that it "only works on pages where the snippet loads" (Appcues docs). Frigade takes a third route: "Train on staging. Install the SDK in prod", after the customer sends "a URL and a test login", plus a public demo that needs no signup (Frigade).

The third pattern is a designed developer step. Jimo has a "Share Instructions to Engineer" button, tells the buyer to "Ask for a date", offers the install as two tabs (a prompt for an AI coding agent, or manual), shows the snippet "as detected on your domain", and extends the trial: "the trial lasts 2 weeks, plus 2 more weeks once the snippet is installed" (Jimo install; Jimo installation settings; Jimo billing). Userflow offers "Copy all instructions (to send to a developer)", writes a self-contained install specification file when its AI skill has no code access, and reports installation, initialisation and user identification as three separate checks (Userflow install with AI; Userflow installation check). Plausible generates its own test traffic and, on failure, "lets you check again, try another URL, review the installation instructions or verify the installation manually" (Plausible docs). Outside the guidance category, Intercom is the only flow found that covers an install-type question, framework-specific tabs, a "Test your installation" button, server-signed identity for logged-in users and a developer invite in one place (Intercom help).

The judgement on "who is best" is therefore split by job. Jimo has the most complete documented flow for a product like Haptic. Intercom has the most complete install for an app behind a login. Plausible has the best verification, PostHog the most mature automated install, and Chatbase the shortest value-first agent setup. Confidence: moderate. The ranking rests on documentation; real friction such as forced demo calls, email verification timing and work-email rules only shows up hands-on.

The cited paper credits the executor, not the map

What arXiv:2510.04607 measured

The paper is "From Imperative to Declarative: Towards LLM-friendly OS Interfaces for Boosted Computer-Use Agents" by Wang, Li and Chen, published at EuroSys 2026 (arXiv). It concerns agents that operate Windows desktop applications on their own. Its system, called the Declarative Model Interface, has two parts. Offline, an automated crawler clicks through Word, Excel and PowerPoint, records which control reveals which, and turns the result into a tree-like map with one unique path to each control; a pruned version of 15,000 to 30,000 tokens goes into the prompt. At run time, a new command lets the model name the control it wants, and a deterministic executor walks the path and clicks, with no further model calls for the hops in between: "Given a control identifier, DMI deterministically navigates from any current state to that control" (arXiv full text). The evaluation used 27 single-application tasks, each run three times, with GPT-5 and GPT-5-mini.

The middle row is the test of the team's belief, and the authors ran it for exactly that reason: "To determine whether DMI's performance gains stem from its declarative interface or simply from providing a static knowledge base". Their conclusion for the strong model is that the map alone "yields no significant change in performance", "indicating that the declarative interface, not the static knowledge, is the primary source of DMI's effectiveness" (arXiv full text). Three further points change how the paper should be read. First, the weaker model did gain modestly from the map alone, which the authors read as "supplementary topology knowledge can be helpful for models with less general-purpose knowledge". With 81 trials per configuration, that gain is 5 trials and the strong-model difference is 2; the paper reports no confidence intervals or significance tests (the trial arithmetic is the researchers' inference). Second, the "single LLM call" claim means four calls per task of which three are fixed framework overhead, and a successful task still took 239 seconds on average. Per-call latency was "similar to the baseline", so the 39% time saving came entirely from making fewer calls, not faster ones. Third, the authors name Haptic's setting as the one they do not handle: "A canonical example is the Web browser. The main challenge of adapting DMI to Web browsers is the high volatility of Web controls". The offline model also cost under three hours of crawling plus "around 1.5 person-days" of manual work per application and is "version-specific" (arXiv full text).

Why the result does not carry over to Haptic as built

The reasoning runs in five steps. One: in the paper the map pays off because an executor clicks through the hidden hops at no model cost. In Haptic the executor is a person, by design, and the number of clicks is fixed by the app's own menus. The only savings available to Haptic are therefore model calls per human step and seconds per call. Two: Haptic makes one model call per step with a site map in the prompt, which is the middle row of the table, the configuration that showed no gain for a strong model. Three: Haptic's map is weaker than the paper's. It comes from a plain-HTTP crawl with no JavaScript and no login, and the web papers that build site graphs explicitly go beyond that: "Traditional crawlers parse static hyperlinks to discover URL-addressable pages but miss interaction-triggered states" (WebNavigator). For a logged-in app, Haptic's repository says such pages "come back nearly empty", so the map holds little about the screens where users get lost. Four: the paper's "critical lesson is the need for applications to expose globally unique identifiers for controls", and Haptic's element ids such as e12 are valid for one page snapshot only. The widget does compute a key that survives across snapshots, from role, label, icon and link target (elementKey in src/widget/snapshot.ts), but it is used only to describe what changed between two snapshots, it is not unique when two controls share a label, and it is never stored or sent to the server. So no stored route could be reused today. Five: Haptic logs no sessions or transitions, so nothing is learned after install either.

The team's belief that a memory of the site will make Haptic "as fast as possible" is contradicted as stated. What the paper supports is narrower: a map plus a mechanism that skips model calls reduces calls for an autonomous agent on desktop benchmark tasks. Confidence: high on what the paper says, which was read in full text with the numbers checked against its Table 3; moderate on the transfer to Haptic, because no study covers a web app, a logged-in app or a human in the loop.

Four findings from this body of work matter for Haptic. A graph does not guarantee time savings on the web: WebNavigator cut steps by 11–12% and left wall-clock time flat. The researchers' explanation is that finding the target, including an 11-second selection call, replaced the navigation steps it saved, and that most time is spent on the destination page (WebNavigator). A raw map can hurt: adding an unsummarised map lowered one agent's success from 47.9% to 44.8%, and summarising it raised success to 57.4% (WebATLAS). Speed on repeats is real but arrives on the second run: MobileGPT's 62.5% latency cut applies to a task type the system has already executed once (MobileGPT). And nothing here was measured on a customer-specific logged-in application; one paper says so directly: "broader external validity (e.g., enterprise apps) remains to be tested" (WALT).

The human-computer interaction evidence supports Haptic's product choice more than its speed claim. In a 20-person study on Google Sheets and Figma, a copilot that gave step-by-step visual guidance let 11 of 20 participants finish the Sheets task, against none with a copilot that did the task for them, and participants completed 88.5% of the task on average against 35.0% (Khurana et al., CHI 2025). In an earlier 16-person study, text-only help from a language model left users unable to find the controls it named, and they "often followed the LLM's suggestions verbatim, even if they were incorrect" (Khurana et al., IUI 2024). No controlled study was found that measures human task time or error rate for on-screen pointing against text instructions in a live web app.

Where Haptic's speed can actually come from

The levers below are ordered by how little they ask of the customer. The first three ask nothing.

The largest untested lever is seconds per call. Haptic's default is a large model at low reasoning effort. Third-party measurements put time to first token for a non-reasoning small model (Claude Haiku 4.5) at 0.62 to 1.50 seconds depending on the provider, and time to the first answer token for a newer small model at low reasoning effort at 6.28 seconds; the second figure is snippet-level and from a workload unlike Haptic's short prompts (Artificial Analysis: Haiku 4.5; Artificial Analysis: Haiku 5.5 low). The researchers' inference is that Haptic is probably paying mostly for thinking time, not output. That must be measured on Haptic's own prompts, and no public benchmark tests small models on Haptic's actual task, choosing an element from a text description of the page. The risk is lost accuracy. Here the paper's ablation cuts the other way: the weaker model was the one that gained from the map. That yields the part of the team's belief that survives, as a hypothesis to test and not a finding: site knowledge will not make a strong model faster, but it could be what lets a faster, smaller model hold its accuracy.

The second lever is to move the pointer as soon as the target arrives. Haptic's structured output already puts target first; Anthropic documents streaming of tool input as it is generated, which allows the pointer to move on a partial result while the sentence follows (snippet-level) (Claude docs). The third is cache hygiene. Haptic's prompt is already cached, but the default cache lifetime is five minutes, so a low-traffic customer's first step is usually cold; a one-hour lifetime and pre-warming when the widget opens are documented options (Claude docs). Anthropic's guidance for knowledge bases under about 200,000 tokens is to "just include the entire knowledge base in the prompt" with caching, and Haptic's 60,000-character help-docs budget sits well inside that, so no retrieval system is needed yet (Anthropic).

The fourth lever is the one the literature supports for large gains: skip the model on steps already seen. The design assembled from the papers and from browser-automation practice is a per-customer store of verified routes. Each entry maps a goal and a screen to a description of the element to point at, using role, visible name and position in the page structure, not a single selector. Before pointing, the entry is checked against the live page; a match points with no model call; a miss falls back to the normal call; and the correction is written back. The published precedent is Stagehand, whose cache returns "with no LLM inference and no token cost" and "falls back to full inference" when "the recorded selector no longer resolves"; its documentation puts cached replay at roughly 50–100 milliseconds against 1–5 seconds, a docs figure seen through a mirror, not a benchmark (Stagehand docs; Stagehand speed guide mirror). Matching on several attributes is the difference between a route that survives a redesign and one that does not: across new versions of 48 public sites, a single-locator baseline failed to find 27% of 801 elements and a multi-attribute matcher failed on 11% at about 4 milliseconds per lookup (Similo). Haptic has two structural advantages no autonomous agent has, both inferences: the person's click produces a settled page before the next pointer, which is a free check at every step, and every guided session is a demonstration of a real route with the user's goal attached. It also has a disadvantage: a wrong cached pointer is shown to a person, so the threshold for skipping the model must be conservative.

The fifth lever uses the user's reading time. A published system that has a fast model predict the next action reports up to 55% accuracy (snippet-level), which implies that roughly half of steps could be precomputed while the user reads and clicks (Speculative Actions). The sixth is to use stable attributes such as test identifiers wherever a customer's app already has them. Two things are not viable as a primary path in 2026: Chrome's built-in model, which is desktop-only and needs at least 22 GB of free disk, and WebMCP, which is discussed below (Chrome docs).

Levers four and five need routes, and routes have to come from somewhere: an admin walking the app, a recorded demonstration, or sessions after install. That is where the technical question becomes an onboarding question. The belief should be restated as something measurable: with a store of verified routes, what share of pointer steps can be served without a model call, at what pointer accuracy? The literature gives reason to expect a large share on repeat goals, since MobileGPT's memory hit rates on scripted repeats were 70.6–98.6%, and gives no number for help-widget traffic. Confidence: moderate on the direction of levers one and four; low on every size.

"Just add the script tag" is the right production install and the wrong first step

The strongest case against the team's belief has four parts. First, the script tag is not a differentiator; nearly every entrant in the category makes the same claim. Second, it is rarely one step. Pendo, Fullstory, PostHog and Intercom all document Content Security Policy changes for their scripts, and buyers' security teams ask a standard list of privacy questions, including "Do you use customer data to train AI models?" (Pendo CSP; PostHog CSP; Vanta). In practice "one script tag" is one script tag, a policy change and a review, done by people who did not sign up (inference). Third, the only data on products where the buyer is not the installer says about half stall at this step for a month or more, and that figure is snippet-only. Fourth, the script by itself teaches Haptic nothing about the customer's app before users arrive. Haptic's website currently says "During onboarding we read your site and your help docs, so Haptic knows your product before it meets your users." For an app behind a login that sentence is true of the help docs and the public pages and false of the product's screens, because the intake crawl has no login and no JavaScript rendering. That promise overstates what exists and should be reworded or made true.

What still holds is substantial. A script tag is the only route to end users of a customer-facing app; an extension never reaches them. Haptic's install is also smaller than the incumbents'. Userflow, Appcues, Userpilot, Pendo and Amplitude all require a user-identification call as well as the snippet, and Jimo's docs budget "About an hour for the person who edits your app" for both; Haptic needs no identification call, stores no selectors and requires nothing to be authored (Userflow docs; Jimo docs). The site's other claim, that Haptic works behind a login because it "runs in the browser on the page your user is looking at", is accurate. Haptic can also verify faster than analytics tools: its first request from an allowed origin is proof of install within seconds, where Pendo warns that initial data "can take up to two hours" (the first half is an inference about Haptic's design; the second is documented) (Pendo support).

The script tag therefore belongs after the buyer has seen Haptic work on their own app and before go-live, and it should land on a local or staging build first, then production for the customer's staff, then everyone. The exception is the signer who answers "yes, I can install it myself": for that person the install comes straight after signup, as it does at Sentry and PostHog. Confidence: high that the tag is table stakes and that five guidance vendors show the product before install; moderate that reordering will raise Haptic's install rate, which no source has tested for this kind of product.

Conclusion

Onboarding and speed turned out to be one design problem, not two. The only kind of memory the evidence credits with large gains is a store of executable, verified routes, and for an app behind a login those routes can only come from people: an admin showing Haptic where things are before launch, and end users being guided after it. The step in the flow where a buyer tests their top questions and corrects the pointer is therefore not a courtesy before the "real" install. It is where Haptic acquires the knowledge its website already claims to have, where the buyer decides whether to trust it, and where the material for model-free steps is first collected. A flow modelled on Crisp would skip that step, because Crisp can get everything it needs from a public URL.

The research also changes what Haptic can claim. "A memory of the site makes it fast" should become a hypothesis with two numbers attached, the share of steps served without a model call and the pointer accuracy at that share, and "one script tag" should be treated as the price of entry, with the difference made by what happens before and after it. Haptic's real structural advantages are untested ones: a person's click verifies every step for free, and every guided session is a labelled demonstration in the real app with real permissions. No published system has measured either, which means the first five customers will produce evidence that does not exist anywhere else, provided each stage of their journey is written down.
