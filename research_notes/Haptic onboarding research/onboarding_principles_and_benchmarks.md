## 1. Definitions: what "onboarding" covers, user versus account onboarding, service models, and the standard metrics

### Takeaway
There is no single authoritative definition. The most-cited practitioners agree that onboarding runs from before signup to an established habit, that "activation" is the measurable milestone inside it, and that in B2B the unit that must activate is the account or team, not the individual who signed up. Setup (which includes any technical install) is explicitly distinguished from the "aha" moment, and treating setup completion as value delivered is named as a common mistake.

### Cited Findings

**Onboarding (scope)**
- **[framework/opinion]** Samuel Hulick (UserOnboard), in a 15 January 2015 Intercom interview: "I define it as any time there's an opportunity to increase the likelihood that users are successful." He places the start before signup ("you can start helping people become successful long before they sign up") and gives no end point. He criticises tooltip tours as "not necessarily accomplishment-oriented" and frames success as "are people making progress in the goal that they were hoping to when they signed up for your product?" *(fetched)* — [Intercom interview with Samuel Hulick](https://www.intercom.com/blog/podcasts/user-onboarding-interview-samuel-hulick/)
- **[framework/opinion]** Ramli John and Wes Bush (*Product-Led Onboarding*, excerpt published 4 June 2021): three success milestones — users complete signup, users experience value for the first time, users begin to use the product consistently — named the "Moment of Value Perception, Moment of Value Experience, and Moment of Value Adoption." Their EUREKA process: Establish a cross-functional onboarding team, Understand why users signed up, Refine success milestones, Evaluate the journey to remove or delay steps, Keep users engaged with in-app and external triggers, Apply and repeat. "User onboarding is a continuous process." *(fetched)* — [ProductLed, EUREKA framework](https://productled.com/blog/the-eureka-framework-to-improve-user-onboarding)
- **[framework/opinion]** Wes Bush's Bowling Alley Framework (published 25 August 2023, updated 28 October 2024) has three phases: (1) build the "straight line" ("the shortest distance to get from Point A to Point B"), labelling every step green (necessary), yellow (delay) or red (remove); (2) add product bumpers — welcome messages, product tours, progress bars, onboarding checklists, tooltips, empty states; (3) add conversational bumpers — email, push, explainer videos, SMS, including "sales touches". *(fetched)* — [ProductLed, Bowling Alley](https://productled.com/blog/product-led-user-onboarding-experience)
- **[first-party definition]** Sentry (2 November 2017) defines onboarding for an SDK-installed product as "simply the process of signing-up, creating a new project, and sending data to it for the first time", and tracks "First Event" (a project has sent a test or real event). *(fetched)* — [Sentry blog](https://blog.sentry.io/small-data-big-insights)

**Activation, setup, aha, habit**
- **[framework/opinion]** Lenny Rachitsky and Yuriy Timen (25 October 2022): "activation rate = [users who hit your activation milestone] / [users who completed your signup flow]"; the milestone is "the earliest point in your onboarding flow that, by showing your product's value, is predictive of long-term retention", and users who hit it should retain "at least 2x better". About 6% of survey respondents reported a time-bound activation definition, with a median window of 10 days and a mode of 7 days (the authors say this undercounts). *(fetched)* — [Lenny's Newsletter, What is a good activation rate](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/what-is-a-good-activation-rate)
- **[framework/opinion]** Lenny Rachitsky (8 November 2022): "A good activation metric is causal for your retention, not just correlative." Process: brainstorm candidate moments, run a regression against retention, then run experiments to test whether moving the metric moves retention. *(fetched; the company-by-company list is paywalled)* — [Lenny's Newsletter, How to determine your activation metric](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/how-to-determine-your-activation)
- **[framework/opinion]** Elena Verna (5 October 2023), quoting the Reforge definition: "Activation is taking a user from signing up to establishing a habit around your core value prop." Three stages: **Setup** ("the user performing the necessary actions to get to the core value prop"), **Aha** ("when the user has received and realized the core value"; it "cannot be just a 'login' or 'view' action"), and **Habit** (first repeat loop at the product's natural frequency). She names as a mistake: "Many companies excel at innovating in setup and mistakenly want to count setup as their value delivery, or aha moment." *(fetched)* — [Elena Verna, Hey B2B, I bet you are measuring activation wrong](https://www.elenaverna.com/p/hey-b2b-i-bet-you-are-measuring-activation)
- **[first-party definition]** PostHog (6 February 2025) defines activation as "when a user experiences your product's value for the first time", notes it is "rarely a single event", and publishes its own per-product criteria, for example product analytics: "first team event ingested, 1 dashboard created, and 3 insights saved within 30 days"; session replay: "5 recordings analyzed and 1 recording list filters changed within 14 days". *(fetched)* — [PostHog newsletter, WTF is activation](https://posthog.com/newsletter/wtf-is-activation)
- **[first-party definition]** PostHog's handbook models activation as three linked stages per product: a product intent (up-funnel), meeting activation criteria within 30 days of intent, and a retention event about three months after intent. *(fetched)* — [PostHog handbook, per-product activation](https://posthog.com/handbook/growth/growth-engineering/per-product-activation)
- **[framework/opinion]** Kyle Poyar (then OpenView), 1 August 2022: "Product activation really just means that you're solving the problem for a user"; activation metrics "are going to be different for every product." *(fetched)* — [Amplitude blog with OpenView](https://www.amplitude.com/blog/benchmark-product-led-growth)

**User onboarding versus account (company) onboarding**
- **[framework/opinion]** Elena Verna: "you need to activate teams in order to be able to sell to companies"; at Miro "we didn't consider an account activated unless there was collaboration" (aha defined as two or more people collaborating on a board). She also lists "requiring a new account for every sign-up instead of matching existing organizations" as a mistake. *(fetched)* — [Elena Verna](https://www.elenaverna.com/p/hey-b2b-i-bet-you-are-measuring-activation)
- **[first-party definition]** PostHog measures activation at the organization level ("Check if an organization meets the activation definition within 30 days of showing product intent") and includes a team-level install signal ("first team event ingested"). *(fetched)* — [PostHog handbook](https://posthog.com/handbook/growth/growth-engineering/per-product-activation); [PostHog newsletter](https://posthog.com/newsletter/wtf-is-activation)

**Self-serve, sales-assisted, white-glove**
- **[benchmark survey]** In the 2026 ChartMogul / Growth Unhinged / ProductLed survey (200 software products, January 2026), "80% of free trial products have human touchpoints when an enterprise user enters the trial" and "70% of freemium products have human touchpoints when an enterprise user self-serves." Pure self-serve is the minority pattern once the account is large. *(fetched)* — [ChartMogul, The SaaS Conversion Report](https://chartmogul.com/reports/saas-conversion-report/)
- **[framework/opinion]** Elena Verna lists "relying only on self-serve activation for enterprise, complex, or high-value accounts" as a common mistake. *(fetched)* — [Elena Verna](https://www.elenaverna.com/p/hey-b2b-i-bet-you-are-measuring-activation)
- **[first-party account]** White-glove ("concierge") onboarding as practised by Superhuman: mandatory 1:1 calls, initially up to 90 minutes (30 discovery + 60 onboarding), later 30 minutes; a full-time specialist ran 8–12 half-hour calls a day at a fully loaded cost of $60k–$130k per year. *(fetched)* — [First Round Review, Superhuman onboarding playbook (Gaurav Vohra)](https://review.firstround.com/superhuman-onboarding-playbook/)

**Standard metrics**
- **Signup-to-activation rate**: defined above (Lenny/Timen). — [Lenny's Newsletter](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/what-is-a-good-activation-rate)
- **Time to value (TTV)**: Userpilot reports an average of "1 day, 12 hours, and 23 minutes"; its page does not state which start and end events it measures between. *(fetched)* — [Userpilot onboarding benchmarks](https://userpilot.com/blog/saas-onboarding-benchmarks/)
- **Time to first event / install**: Sentry's "First Event"; PostHog's "first event in 1.9 hours vs 3.8". *(fetched)* — [Sentry](https://blog.sentry.io/small-data-big-insights); [PostHog](https://posthog.com/newsletter/context-engineering)
- **Setup / checklist completion rate**: Userpilot reports "onboarding checklist completion rate" (average 19.2%). *(fetched)* — [Userpilot](https://userpilot.com/blog/product-metrics-benchmark-report/)
- **Free-to-paid conversion**: ChartMogul/Growth Unhinged define it as the share of free signups who become paying customers "within six months". *(fetched)* — [ChartMogul](https://chartmogul.com/reports/saas-conversion-report/)
- **PQL (product-qualified lead)**: an example trigger from OpenView's Kyle Poyar is "As soon as you hit five users in the same account." *(fetched)* — [Amplitude blog with OpenView](https://www.amplitude.com/blog/benchmark-product-led-growth)

### Inferences
- For a product installed by one person and evaluated by another, "user activation" and "account activation" are different events performed by different people. The account-level definition (as at Miro and PostHog) is the one that fits: for example, "script live on at least one environment AND N end-user questions answered within X days".
- Under the Reforge/Verna vocabulary, "script installed" is a **setup** moment, not the aha. The aha is the first time the buyer sees the product working on their own app.
- Hulick's "starts before signup" definition makes the landing page, the request-access form and any pre-signup demo part of onboarding.

### Gaps
- I could not source Sean Ellis's own definitions (aha moment, must-have experience, *Hacking Growth*) or Dave McClure's AARRR "Activation" definition, because the search budget ran out. They are widely credited but I have no verified URL.
- I did not find a primary Reforge page; the Reforge definition is quoted via Elena Verna. Secondary sources disagree on whether Reforge names three or four moments and whether to credit Brian Balfour, Casey Winters or Shaun Clowes.
- The origin of "PQL" (often attributed to OpenView or Tomasz Tunguz, circa 2013–2016) is not confirmed. A "PQLs convert at 25–30%" figure circulates and is attributed to OpenView/Gainsight; I found no primary source and it should not be repeated.
- OpenView's own benchmark pages now return the firm's home page or 404, so OpenView definitions are cited through third parties.
- No authoritative source found for the "high-touch / low-touch / tech-touch" taxonomy with ACV thresholds.
- "Install rate" has no standard published definition; products define it themselves (first event, SDK initialized, snippet detected).

## 2. Benchmarks with numbers: activation, conversion, checklist completion, time to value

### Takeaway
The credible central estimate for self-reported B2B SaaS activation is a median of about 25–37% of signups, with every source using its own definition. Developer-facing products convert free users to paid at roughly half the rate of other SaaS (5% versus 10% within six months in OpenView's 2023 data). Onboarding checklists are completed by about one in five users in the one vendor dataset that reports it. None of these numbers is definition-consistent, so they are useful as order-of-magnitude anchors and not as targets.

### Inferences
- The Lenny (median 25–30%) and Userpilot (median 37%) figures are not in conflict; they measure different self-selected populations with different milestones. A fair summary is "roughly a quarter to a third of signups activate at the median".
- Userpilot's finding that sales-led companies activate more users than PLG companies (41.6% versus 34.6%) and OpenView's finding that human outreach doubles conversion and improves retention 2–3x point in the same direction: human help raises activation. Both are correlational.
- Amplitude's 2.1% median is a return-on-day-7 measure across all new users of mostly large consumer-scale products. It should not be put in the same table column as the 25–37% milestone-based figures without that warning.
- Sentry's 70% is a ceiling case for an install-gated product, because Sentry's signups are developers who can install it themselves. A product whose signups are mostly non-engineers should expect far less at the install step.

### Gaps
- No benchmark of activation rate specifically for developer or infrastructure products was found. The boldstart analysis explicitly declines to give one.
- OpenView's 2020 figures (median activation about 20% for freemium and 40% for free trial; free-to-paid about 7% versus 14%) appeared only in a search snippet of an OpenView blog that now redirects to the firm's home page. Treat as unverified.
- First Page Sage's opt-in versus opt-out trial figures (18.2% versus 48.8%, 86 companies, Q1 2022–Q3 2025) were seen only through aggregator blogs. Not verified at the primary source.
- Appcues, Userflow, Pendo and Mixpanel benchmark reports with onboarding-specific numbers and methodology were not located. Mixpanel's 2024 Benchmarks Report (7,700 customers) surfaced only in summaries without B2B activation figures.
- Userpilot does not publish its methodology, and its sample size is stated inconsistently (62 versus 547).
- A widely repeated statistic has no traceable primary source: "40% to 60% of users who sign up will never return." ProductLed's EUREKA article links it to an Intercom page ([intercom.com/blog/onboarding-guide](https://www.intercom.com/blog/onboarding-guide)); I fetched that page and it contains no such statement. Wes Bush's own article states it without a source. It should not be presented as data.
- Other unsourced figures seen on vendor pages that should not be repeated as evidence: "up to 75% of new users may leave within the first week" (Appcues), users who complete onboarding are "10 times more likely to become paying customers" and "well over 30% of required user onboarding steps are rubbish" (ProductLed).

## 3. The technical-install cliff: how many signups complete a snippet or SDK install, and what raises it

### Takeaway
I found no published benchmark for the share of signups that complete a snippet or SDK install; this is the most important missing number. The one figure I found is that almost half of one snippet product's signups (Upscope) had still not installed after a month, against a best case of about 70% reaching first event within two days at Sentry, whose signups are developers. The largest reported lifts come from removing the work from the human (AI or CLI install wizards: about 3x installs at DevCycle; faster and higher activation at PostHog) and from an explicit "can you install this yourself?" fork with a one-click email to a developer (Upscope halved its non-install rate). Every one of these is a self-reported, mostly uncontrolled before/after.

### Cited Findings

**How big the cliff is**
- **[first-party experiment]** Upscope (co-browsing product installed by snippet): "almost half did not have Upscope installed after one month". *(search snippet only; the post now redirects in a loop and could not be fetched)* — [Upscope blog](https://blog.upscope.com/this-simple-intercom-email-trick-helped-us-double-installations)
- **[first-party, SDK product]** Sentry (developer signups): about 70% of users on its best two platforms reach First Event within two days; other platforms lower. *(fetched)* — [Sentry blog](https://blog.sentry.io/small-data-big-insights)
- **[first-party account]** DevCycle (feature flags), 6 October 2025: with its previous tutorial-based onboarding "most users skipped the flow", and those who finished "still had to figure out the SDK install on their own". *(fetched)* — [DevCycle blog](https://blog.devcycle.com/we-rebuilt-our-onboarding-around-mcp-the-result-3x-sdk-installs/)
- **[vendor article]** Appcues, 2 June 2026: "Analytics tools face a steep wall: nothing works until someone installs a tracking snippet or SDK." *(fetched)* — [Appcues, SaaS onboarding examples](https://www.appcues.com/blog/saas-user-onboarding)
- **[opinion]** John Chen (Fika Ventures) in TechCrunch, 27 May 2021, lists as a flow-stopping moment: "I'm a project manager, but I need help from the engineering or data teams to deploy this snippet into the code base." No numbers. *(fetched)* — [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2021/05/27/saas-needs-to-take-a-page-out-of-the-crypto-playbook)

**The "buyer is not the installer" handoff**
- **[first-party experiment]** Upscope's change: after signup, ask whether the user can install the product themselves; those who say yes see the code; those who say no get a prominent button to send their developer an email in one click, with Upscope cc'd so it can answer the developer's questions directly. Result claimed: the change "cut that number in half" (the share not installed after a month), which the title describes as doubling installations. No sample size or control. *(search snippet only)* — [Upscope blog](https://blog.upscope.com/this-simple-intercom-email-trick-helped-us-double-installations)
- **[docs]** Intercom's Messenger install page lets a buyer invite a developer: "enter their email address, and an invite will be sent allowing them to join your workspace with the minimum permissions required to install the Messenger." The invitee receives three permissions (general and security settings, Messenger settings, workspace data). Install options are grouped as custom code (snippet, React, Angular, Vue, Ember) and integrations (WordPress, Google Tag Manager, Shopify, Segment). *(fetched)* — [Intercom help, new Messenger install experience](https://www.intercom.com/help/en/articles/16946116-new-messenger-install-experience-on-your-website)
- **[first-party design account]** Optimizely's mobile onboarding redesign split the flow so that the product manager and the developer each got their own path, after user research showed developers were influential in adoption. *(search snippet only; the Medium page returned 403)* — [Optimizely design blog](https://medium.com/design-optimizely/behind-the-design-of-optimizely-s-mobile-onboarding-c47ad1d2748e)
- **[first-party experiment]** Sentry added a platform (language/framework) question at signup in its 2017 redesign so that install instructions could be tailored. *(fetched)* — [Sentry blog](https://blog.sentry.io/small-data-big-insights)
- **[first-party experiment]** Twilio (10 September 2025) added an intake survey at first login asking role, code versus no-code preference, and use case, which forks the flow; no-code users are routed to partners. Combined with other changes it reported "a 62% improvement in first message activation" (see below). *(fetched)* — [Twilio blog](https://www.twilio.com/en-us/blog/developers/redesigning-twilio-onboarding-experience-whats-new)

**Install verification**
- **[docs]** Intercom: "Click Test your installation to confirm the Messenger is working as expected"; on success the user sees a "Messenger is installed" confirmation. *(fetched)* — [Intercom help](https://www.intercom.com/help/en/articles/16946116-new-messenger-install-experience-on-your-website)

"The Quick Start content is also used by our in-app onboarding wizard." *(fetched)* — [Sentry docs, writing a quick start](https://docs.sentry.io/contributing/approach/sdk-docs/write-quick-start)

- **[first-party experiment]** PostHog, 24 June 2026 (Edwin Lim): the PostHog Wizard is "an AI agent that fully integrates PostHog into any codebase with a single CLI command" (`npx @posthog/wizard`). Reported: "14.2% of wizard users convert to paid usage vs 2.6%"; "first event in 1.9 hours vs 3.8, with 94% activating within one hour vs 67%"; "80% larger MRR in the first paying month". The post gives no sample size, period, assignment method or definition of the comparison group, so self-selection (motivated developers choose the wizard) is not ruled out. *(fetched)* — [PostHog newsletter, context engineering](https://posthog.com/newsletter/context-engineering)
- **[first-party experiment]** DevCycle, 6 October 2025: onboarding rebuilt as three steps inside the developer's AI coding assistant (install the MCP server; prompt "Install the DevCycle SDK."; prompt to create a flagged Hello World). Reported "~3× more users reach SDK install" versus the previous flow. No absolute rates, sample or period; the authors add "We're obviously being careful here: onboarding metrics evolve as traffic and cohorts change" and note "AI editing code can make people nervous." *(fetched)* — [DevCycle blog](https://blog.devcycle.com/we-rebuilt-our-onboarding-around-mcp-the-result-3x-sdk-installs/)
- **[docs]** Sentry: "The wizard is the fastest way to set up Sentry." *(fetched)* — [Sentry docs](https://docs.sentry.io/contributing/approach/sdk-docs/write-quick-start)

**Guided, personalised setup**
- **[first-party experiment]** Twilio, 10 September 2025: the redesigned onboarding (intake survey, personalised "For You" page, auto-selected configuration, single-screen configure/test/logs, pre-loaded runnable code samples, save and resume, and a "Virtual Phone" that receives test messages without carrier registration) delivered "a 62% improvement in first message activation" and "a 33% improvement in production launches within 7 days" versus users shown the old experience. Described as "Early results"; no baseline, sample or statement of whether the change is relative or absolute. *(fetched)* — [Twilio blog](https://www.twilio.com/en-us/blog/developers/redesigning-twilio-onboarding-experience-whats-new)

**Doing the install with the customer**
- **[founder essay]** Paul Graham, July 2013: "At YC we use the term 'Collison installation' for the technique they invented." "When anyone agreed to try Stripe they'd say 'Right then, give me your laptop' and set them up on the spot." *(fetched)* — [Paul Graham, Do Things That Don't Scale](https://paulgraham.com/ds.html)
- **[benchmark survey, secondary analysis]** In OpenView's 2023 dev-tool data, companies whose sales teams reach out to more than 50% of new signups convert roughly twice as well as those that do not. *(fetched)* — [boldstart](https://boldstart.vc/?p=1596)

**Tag-manager installs**
- **[docs]** Intercom offers Google Tag Manager and Segment as install paths that do not require an application code change. *(fetched)* — [Intercom help](https://www.intercom.com/help/en/articles/16946116-new-messenger-install-experience-on-your-website)
- **[docs]** PostHog's security guidance treats tag-manager installs as a weaker-control path: "Don't combine this setup with the snippet, tag-manager installations, or another permissively configured SDK copy." *(fetched)* — [PostHog docs, securely install the JavaScript SDK](https://posthog.com/docs/libraries/js/security)

### Inferences
- The only quantified handoff experiment (Upscope) and the only quantified wizard experiments (PostHog, DevCycle) are all small-company or uncontrolled self-reports. The direction is consistent; the magnitudes are not reliable.
- Three distinct levers appear, in rising order of reported effect: (a) make instructions specific to the stack (Sentry's platform question); (b) route the task to the right person with the least possible friction (Upscope's email-to-developer with vendor cc'd, Intercom's minimum-permission invite); (c) remove the manual work (wizard or AI assistant that edits the codebase).
- For a logged-in web app, a tag-manager path is less likely to be available than for a marketing site, and security teams may prefer a reviewed code change. That is my inference from the PostHog guidance, not a measured fact.
- A vendor cc'd on the developer hand-off email (Upscope) is a cheap way to turn a silent failure point into a support conversation.

### Gaps
- **No published share-of-signups-that-install figure** was found for Segment, Hotjar, Mixpanel, Amplitude, Heap, FullStory, LaunchDarkly, Stripe, Algolia or Intercom. A targeted search returned only help-centre pages. This number appears to be treated as proprietary.
- Appcues's well-known post "We increased our activation rate by 2.5x" (which I believe concerned letting users build before installing) now resolves to an unrelated 2026 article; the original could not be retrieved, so it is not cited.
- No first-party experiment was found on framework-specific instructions in isolation, on install verification in isolation, or on tag-manager installs.
- Hotjar, Heap, FullStory, LogRocket and Pendo documentation of "send instructions to a developer" and "verify installation" could not be checked because the search budget ran out; the patterns are documented here only for Intercom and Sentry.
- A commonly repeated claim that Stripe targets a "time to first API call" under 90 seconds (or under 5 minutes) comes only from marketing blogs; no primary Stripe source was found.
- A third-party teardown of PostHog's current onboarding (Indie Hackers) claims non-technical product managers close the tab at the terminal-command step; the page returned 403 and the claim is anecdotal.

## 4. Value before install: demo data, sandboxes, playgrounds, reverse trials, interactive demos, versus gating

### Takeaway
The direction of the evidence favours letting the buyer experience something real before the hard step, but almost all of it is vendor self-report with self-selection. The clearest patterns are a simulated or sandboxed version of the core experience (Twilio's Virtual Phone, Chameleon's no-install sandbox, Mixpanel's demo project, Stripe's test mode) and ungated product access. There is also evidence in the other direction: Superhuman found that an interruptive, full-screen, blocking setup step raised completion from 30% to over 98% when the step was clearly valuable. I found no experiment on "waiting for your first event" screens specifically.

### Cited Findings
- **[first-party experiment, vendor]** Chameleon (in-app guidance product that itself requires a snippet): "We created a sandbox environment where users could explore Chameleon's features without installing any code." "Users who explored the sandbox reached key milestones 25% faster than those who had to install the code upfront." Also: "users who engaged with an interactive demo before diving into the product activated at a rate of 50.85%—a ~24% increase over the original 41.2%"; they published a first experience at 13.6% versus 4.6%; and converted to paid at 3.4% versus 1.6%. These compare users who chose to engage with users who did not, so motivation is a confound. Undated page; no sample sizes. *(fetched)* — [Chameleon, Auditing onboarding](https://www.chameleon.io/shift-left/auditing-onboarding)
- **[first-party experiment, negative result]** Chameleon's front-loaded onboarding email sequence barely moved activation: "Publishing an experience saw a very small lift, from 8.3% to 8.5%"; lesson stated: "users were more responsive to in-app prompts and suggestions rather than receiving an overload of emails." *(fetched)* — [Chameleon](https://www.chameleon.io/shift-left/auditing-onboarding)
- **[first-party experiment]** Twilio's Virtual Phone is a console test handset that accepts messages through real APIs before carrier registration is approved: "We created a Virtual Phone in the console so that you can build your prototype." It shipped as part of the redesign that reported a 62% improvement in first-message activation. *(fetched)* — [Twilio blog](https://www.twilio.com/en-us/blog/developers/redesigning-twilio-onboarding-experience-whats-new)
- **[vendor article]** Mixpanel lets new users "explore a demo project with pre-populated data" before implementation; Stripe's test mode and pre-filled code samples get developers to a successful test payment without real payment infrastructure. Appcues's stated lesson: "give people a way to experience the product before that prerequisite is done"; "Templates and sample data remove the blank-canvas problem." Descriptive, no outcome data. *(fetched)* — [Appcues](https://www.appcues.com/blog/saas-user-onboarding)
- **[benchmark survey]** Ungated products (usable before account creation): Kyle Poyar reports "I've seen as much as a 3x increase in the number of website visitors who start using an ungated product", with slightly fewer creating an account than in classic freemium. In the 200-product survey, ungated freemium converted 7% of visitors to signup and 8% of signups to paid, versus 9% and 5.5% for regular freemium. *(fetched)* — [ChartMogul, The SaaS Conversion Report](https://chartmogul.com/reports/saas-conversion-report/)
- **[benchmark survey]** Reverse trials: "good" 4–6% and "great" 8–12% free-to-paid, with the report noting the sample was small and the difference from freemium not statistically significant. *(fetched)* — [ChartMogul](https://chartmogul.com/reports/saas-conversion-report/)
- **[first-party experiment, vendor]** Chameleon's reverse enterprise trial: "the conversion rate from trial to paid users increased by 50%." No baseline given. *(fetched)* — [Chameleon](https://www.chameleon.io/shift-left/auditing-onboarding)
- **[vendor telemetry]** Interactive demos (Navattic, 2026; 40,000+ demos built on its platform, each with at least 100 engaged users): top-10% demos see 53% engagement, 40% completion and 32% click-through; "18% out of 5k B2B SaaS websites have an interactive demo CTA" (up from 12% in 2024); "Interactive demos have a 12% higher conversion rate than product videos"; flows with the highest completion had 1–6 steps. No data on downstream trial activation. *(fetched)* — [Navattic, State of the Interactive Product Demo 2026](https://navattic.com/report/state-of-the-interactive-product-demo-2026)
- **[first-party experiment, gating can help]** Superhuman: making a setup step full-screen and interruptive moved completion "from 30% to over 98%" and key feature opt-ins "from 45% to nearly 80%"; focusing self-serve onboarding on reaching Inbox Zero "improved [self-serve activation] by 25%, from 40% to 50%". Stated principle: be interruptive "but only for something extremely valuable". *(fetched)* — [First Round Review, Superhuman](https://review.firstround.com/superhuman-onboarding-playbook/)
- **[first-party, optional help is skipped]** Superhuman: when the onboarding call went from mandatory to optional, attendance fell "from 100%" to a typical 15%. *(fetched)* — [First Round Review](https://review.firstround.com/superhuman-onboarding-playbook/)
- **[framework/opinion]** Elena Verna calls oversimplifying onboarding an "old myth" and states that a "3 screen/9 question onboarding has no more than a 10% drop-off rate" (her experience, no dataset). *(fetched)* — [Elena Verna](https://www.elenaverna.com/p/hey-b2b-i-bet-you-are-measuring-activation)
- **[first-party experiment]** Databox improved activation "from 30% to 40%+" over four onboarding experiments (Growth Unhinged, 28 February 2024, by Yaakov Carno). The detail is paywalled. *(fetched, headline only)* — [Growth Unhinged, The evolution of product onboarding at Databox](https://www.growthunhinged.com/p/the-evolution-of-product-onboarding-databox)

### Inferences
- The evidence separates two questions that are often merged: (1) should the user see value before the hard step? (mostly yes); (2) should steps be blocking or skippable? (blocking works when the step is the value; optional steps get skipped by about 85% at Superhuman).
- For a product whose value appears only inside the customer's own logged-in app, generic demo data is a weak substitute, because the aha is "it works on my app". The closest analogues in the evidence are Twilio's Virtual Phone (the real API against a simulated endpoint) and Chameleon's sandbox. A playground that runs the real product against the customer's real content, before the script is installed, fits this pattern. This is my inference; no source tested it for this product type.
- A blocking "waiting for your first event" screen is the Superhuman interruptive pattern applied to a step the signer often cannot perform. By analogy with Upscope, it is likely to help developer signers and strand non-developer signers unless it carries a hand-off action. Inference only.

### Gaps
- No experiment was found that isolates a blocking "waiting for first event" screen against a skippable one.
- No controlled study was found comparing demo data against no demo data on activation. Figures circulating on vendor blogs (for example "48% conversion lift with demo data", "2.3x activation") have no stated method and are excluded.
- Navattic compares interactive demos with videos, not with no demo, and reports nothing on post-signup activation.
- I did not find Kyle Poyar's or Elena Verna's primary reverse-trial write-ups; a "38% higher conversion than freemium" figure attributed to Verna and Amplitude could not be traced.

## 5. Signup friction: fields, SSO, email verification, work email, credit card, and segmentation questions

### Takeaway
Removing signup friction raises signups, but the better-evidenced point is that friction which routes or qualifies can leave total paying customers unchanged or higher. Card-required trials produce about three times as many paying customers per visitor as free trials overall in the 2026 ChartMogul survey (10.5 versus 3.6 per 1,000 visitors), a work-email nudge raised work-email share by a third at no cost to conversion at Rows, and role and use-case questions were part of Twilio's large reported activation gain. One-click Google sign-in is the most consistently supported friction reducer.

### Cited Findings
- **[vendor telemetry, correlational]** Heap, 20 March 2017 (79 SaaS companies, signup-page-to-submit conversion over three months): "The average was 36.2%"; with one-click third-party OAuth signup "the signup rates improved by 8.2 percentage points"; "extra form fields like company name, job title, and phone number hurt conversion"; splitting the form across pages "was correlated with a modest increase in conversion". Old and observational. *(fetched)* — [Heap, What's a good conversion rate for a signup flow](https://heap.io/blog/good-conversion-rate-signup-flow)
- **[first-party, uncontrolled]** Clicky, 21 April 2026: after adding Sign in with Google, "we saw an average 40% increase in new accounts per day, vs the same period one month prior" over 14 days. Month-over-month comparison with a holiday in the window; no downstream activation data. *(fetched)* — [Clicky blog](https://clicky.com/blog/google-sign-in-sso-new-user-conversion)
- **[first-party experiment, pre/post]** Rows, 31 March 2023 (four weeks before versus four weeks after; sample sizes not reported): a nudge toward company email produced a "33% increase in the share of users registering with a work email" and "had no adverse impact on website conversion rate, which remained at 5-6%"; adding a use-case question left onboarding completion "very similar" (91% versus 93%) but taught little, since "'Not sure' or 'Other' options were the most popular options, accounting for 25% of all selections"; an invite-teammates step was used by "~1% of new sign-ups". *(fetched)* — [Rows blog](https://rows.com/blog/post/experimenting-with-questions-and-hints-in-the-user-onboarding)
- **[first-party experiment]** Twilio's intake survey (role, code versus no-code, use case) drives a personalised page and auto-selected configuration; the bundle reported a 62% improvement in first-message activation. The survey's individual contribution is not isolated. *(fetched)* — [Twilio blog](https://www.twilio.com/en-us/blog/developers/redesigning-twilio-onboarding-experience-whats-new)
- **[framework/opinion]** Elena Verna: a "3 screen/9 question onboarding has no more than a 10% drop-off rate". *(fetched)* — [Elena Verna](https://www.elenaverna.com/p/hey-b2b-i-bet-you-are-measuring-activation)
- **[benchmark survey]** Credit card up front (ChartMogul / Growth Unhinged 2026, 200 products): card-required trials sign up 3.5% of visitors and convert 30% of them (about 10.5 customers per 1,000 visitors) versus 4.5% and 8% for all trials (about 3.6). The report warns "introducing a credit card requirement could blunt signups" and only 20% of trial products require one, so product type is a confound. *(fetched)* — [ChartMogul](https://chartmogul.com/reports/saas-conversion-report/)
- **[vendor case, unquantified outcome]** Email verification: "27% of all Snappa's signups never ended up activating their email address", after which "Snappa removed the activation step right at the beginning of the onboarding process"; MRR is said to have grown, with no figures. ProductLed also asserts "We've found that 30-50% of signups don't verify their email", unsourced. *(fetched)* — [ProductLed, onboarding examples](https://productled.com/blog/best-user-onboarding-examples)
- **[first-party, single case]** Theodo (2022, a healthcare app): 15% of users who reached the email-verification screen dropped; letting users verify after signup produced about a 15% increase in the onboarding funnel. *(search snippet only; the URL now redirects to the blog index)* — [Theodo blog](https://blog.theodo.com/2022/06/improving-user-onboarding-funnel)
- **[benchmark survey]** The most-mentioned ways to raise activation among 500+ respondents were "Simpler onboarding UI/UX" and "Reducing onboarding friction", ahead of emails, copy and sales outreach. *(fetched)* — [Lenny's Newsletter](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/what-is-a-good-activation-rate)
- **[framework/opinion]** Wes Bush's rule for every signup and setup step: green (keep), yellow (delay), red (remove). *(fetched)* — [ProductLed](https://productled.com/blog/product-led-user-onboarding-experience)

### Inferences
- "Fewer fields is always better" is supported for signup-form conversion (Heap) but not for activation or revenue. The experiments that measured downstream effects (Rows, ChartMogul card data, Twilio) show qualifying or routing steps costing little or nothing.
- A role question at signup is justified when the answer changes what happens next. Rows's use-case question changed nothing downstream and produced 25% "Not sure/Other"; Twilio's role and code/no-code question forks the flow. The lesson is to ask only what you will branch on.
- For a B2B product that needs a domain and a script on a company app, a work-email nudge (not a hard block) has the best available support.

### Gaps
- No controlled experiment was found on requiring (rather than nudging) a work email, or on deferring email verification in B2B SaaS with activation as the outcome.
- No rigorous public A/B test was found showing that adding a personalisation question raised activation in isolation. Vendor claims such as "41% activation lift" and "156% trial-to-paid lift" have no visible method and are excluded.
- Older Google sign-in claims (KISSmetrics "11% to 59.7%", Pinterest "126%", Zapier "20%") reached me only through vendor blogs and are not cited.

## 6. Checklists and progress indicators: the evidence and the psychology usually cited

### Takeaway
The psychology is weaker support than vendor posts suggest. The endowed progress and goal-gradient studies are real and peer-reviewed, but they concern loyalty-card purchases for a tangible reward, and the endowed-progress effect depended on giving a reason for the head start. The Zeigarnik effect failed to hold in a 2025 meta-analysis, and a 2013 meta-analysis of 32 survey experiments found constant progress bars do not reduce drop-off. In the field, about a fifth of users complete onboarding checklists and about a quarter interact with them at all; I found no public controlled test of checklist versus no checklist.

### Cited Findings

**Peer-reviewed psychology**
- **[peer-reviewed]** Nunes and Drèze (2006), "The Endowed Progress Effect: How Artificial Advancement Increases Effort", *Journal of Consumer Research* 32(4), 504–512. Abstract: "people provided with artificial advancement toward a goal exhibit greater persistence toward reaching the goal"; "By converting a task requiring eight steps into a task requiring 10 steps but with two steps already complete," the task is reframed as begun; "This increases the likelihood of task completion and decreases completion time." "Moderators include the reason, if any, offered for the endowment and the currency in which progress is recorded." *(abstract fetched)* — [Journal of Consumer Research](https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article/32/4/504/1787425)
- **[peer-reviewed, via secondary summaries]** The field study involved "300 customers of a car wash who received loyalty cards"; over nine months, 34% of the pre-stamped ten-space cards were completed versus 19% of the unstamped eight-space cards. The effect is "more likely to operate when consumers are provided with a reason for the endowment". The 34% and 19% figures are not in the abstract; they come from summaries. *(fetched)* — [Knowledge at Wharton, 6 September 2006](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/?p=5630); [Coglode summary](https://www.coglode.com/nuggets/endowed-progress-effect)
- **[peer-reviewed]** Kivetz, Urminsky and Zheng (2006), "The Goal-Gradient Hypothesis Resurrected: Purchase Acceleration, Illusionary Goal Progress, and Customer Retention", *Journal of Marketing Research* 43, 39–58: café customers buy more frequently as they near a free coffee; customers given a 12-stamp card with two pre-filled stamps completed the ten purchases faster than those with a 10-stamp card (median 10 versus 15 days, per the search summary of the paper). *(search snippet of the Columbia page)* — [Columbia Business School](https://business.columbia.edu/faculty/research/goal-gradient-hypothesis-resurrected-purchase-acceleration-illusionary-goal)
- **[peer-reviewed meta-analysis]** Ghibellini and Meier (2025), "Interruption, recall and resumption: a meta-analysis of the Zeigarnik and Ovsiankina effects", *Humanities and Social Sciences Communications* 12(1): "We found no memory advantage for unfinished tasks but found a general tendency to resume tasks." "the Ovsiankina effect represents a general tendency, whereas the Zeigarnik effect lacks universal validity." *(abstract fetched)* — [RePEc record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/pal/palcom/v12y2025i1d10.1057_s41599-025-05000-w.html)
- **[peer-reviewed meta-analysis]** Villar, Callegaro and Yang (2013), "Where Am I? A Meta-Analysis of Experiments on the Effects of Progress Indicators for Web Surveys", *Social Science Computer Review* 31, 744–762 (32 randomized experiments): a constant progress indicator "does not significantly help reduce drop-offs"; fast-to-slow indicators reduced drop-off; slow-to-fast indicators increased it; with a small incentive, a constant indicator increased drop-off. *(fetched)* — [Google Research](https://research.google/pubs/where-am-i-a-meta-analysis-of-experiments-on-the-effects-of-progress-indicators-for-web-surveys/)

**Field data on checklists**
- **[vendor telemetry]** "The average checklist completion rate is 19.2%" (Userpilot 2024; FinTech and insurance 24.5%, MarTech 12.5%, PLG 19%, sales-led 22.1%). *(fetched)* — [Userpilot](https://userpilot.com/blog/product-metrics-benchmark-report/); [Userpilot 2026 page](https://userpilot.com/blog/saas-onboarding-benchmarks/)
- **[vendor telemetry]** "Around 23% of users explore and click within a Launcher" (27% with a welcome state); those who engage "complete on average five checklist items per session"; tours launched from a checklist complete at 67% versus 31%; progress indicators "improve completion by 12%" for tours. *(fetched)* — [Chameleon 2025](https://chameleon.io/benchmark-report)
- **[framework/opinion]** Item count: "I recommend having between three and five checklist items for a new user to complete" (Wes Bush). Appcues: keep checklists to "three to five" items and write them as goals such as "Connect your first data source". Neither cites data. *(fetched)* — [ProductLed](https://productled.com/blog/product-led-user-onboarding-experience); [Appcues](https://www.appcues.com/blog/saas-user-onboarding)
- **[first-party experiment]** Superhuman's results favour opinionated, interruptive steps over optional guidance: full-screen setup completion "from 30% to over 98%". *(fetched)* — [First Round Review](https://review.firstround.com/superhuman-onboarding-playbook/)

### Inferences
- What the studies support: a visible head start with a stated reason makes people more likely to finish a short, reward-bearing sequence, and effort rises near the end. What they do not support: that a ten-item software checklist with no tangible reward raises activation. That is an extrapolation by vendors.
- The moderator in Nunes and Drèze matters for design. A pre-checked "Account created" item is a head start with a legitimate reason, which is the condition under which the effect held. An arbitrary "9% complete" with no reason is closer to the condition where it weakened. (The reference checklist described in the brief, "9% complete" with ten items, is arithmetically about one step credited out of eleven, which would be consistent with a single pre-credited step such as account creation. That reading is mine and unverified.)
- Villar et al. imply that a progress display moving slowly at the start discourages. A checklist whose first real item is "wait for an engineer to install a script" front-loads the slowest step and resembles the slow-to-fast case. Putting completable items (train on content, test in playground, invite a developer) before the install item fits the fast-to-slow pattern. This transfer from surveys to onboarding is my inference.
- Zeigarnik should not be cited as support for checklists. The related resumption tendency (Ovsiankina) is the part that held up, and it supports "save and resume" more than "show an unfinished list".
- Field data imply the checklist is seen and used by a minority (about 23% engage, about 19% complete). It is a secondary aid for the motivated, not the primary path. The primary path needs to be the flow itself.
- Vendor guidance (3–5 items) conflicts with the ten-item reference design the team likes. No data settles it.

### Gaps
- No public controlled A/B test of an onboarding checklist versus none, with activation as the outcome, was found.
- No data on pre-checked first items in software onboarding specifically.
- I did not read the full text of Nunes and Drèze or Kivetz et al.; the 34%/19% and 10/15-day figures come from summaries, not from the papers' own pages.
- A blog critique alleges coding errors in the Villar et al. meta-analysis; I could not assess it.
- No HCI-venue (CHI) study of onboarding checklists was located.

## 7. Waitlist or request-access gating versus open self-serve signup for an early-stage B2B product

### Takeaway
The evidence here is founder testimony, not measurement, and it is consistent: gate while you are still learning who the customer is and what trips them up, onboard by hand, then open self-serve when lead volume exceeds what you can handle manually and you can write down what the calls were teaching. Arcade switched after about a year and its first hundred customers; Superhuman took roughly three years to build the self-serve replacement. I found no benchmark for waitlist conversion or decay.

### Cited Findings
- **[founder essay]** Paul Graham, July 2013: "The most common unscalable thing founders have to do at the start is to recruit users manually." "Your first users should feel that signing up with you was one of the best choices they ever made." "you can sometimes get away with doing by hand things that you plan to automate later"; "you'll know exactly what to build because you'll have muscle memory from doing it yourself." On launches: "one sort of initial tactic that usually doesn't work: the Big Launch." The essay names no trigger for stopping. *(fetched)* — [Paul Graham, Do Things That Don't Scale](https://paulgraham.com/ds.html)
- **[first-party account]** Superhuman (Gaurav Vohra): "I believe every founder should spend time manually onboarding their earliest customers." Reasons: customer success, product feedback, loyalty, sidestepping product gaps, resource arbitrage. Each call yielded about "10 pages of notes", 5–10 feature requests and 5–10 bugs. Human-led onboarding saw "nearly 2x" the activation of self-serve (he notes selection bias); "Over 65%" of human-onboarded customers fully moved their email to Superhuman, "more than double" those without; about double the referrals. Switch rule: "We scaled human-led onboarding until the costs, particularly supply and demand mismatch, overtook the benefits." Building the in-product replacement took three years, after which white-glove time was redirected to the highest-value prospects and B2B customers. He also warns that human onboarding is a poor substitute for missing core functionality. *(fetched)* — [First Round Review](https://review.firstround.com/superhuman-onboarding-playbook/)
- **[first-party account]** Arcade (interactive demo software), 18 October 2022: started with a waitlist because "We wanted to have a close relationship with our customers" and "got to know our first one hundred customers very well". Calls revealed that "Users were confused about the first action that they needed to take inside our product." Switch: "Around June 2022, we decided that we knew enough about the customer and GTM strategy to make the investment in self-service"; the team was "not keeping up with leads" and manual emailing of signup links was "clearly a friction point for growth". "Going self-service is not a simple switch to turn on"; it required usability interviews, moving knowledge from email, Intercom and Zoom into the product, and a new onboarding flow replacing the Typeform. Result: "We've seen 10x growth in new inbounds", plus a heavier support load ("10x more receptive to customer questions"). *(fetched)* — [Arcade, Going self-service](https://arcade.software/post/going-self-service)
- **[benchmark survey]** Human involvement persists after self-serve opens: 70–80% of freemium and trial products add human touchpoints for enterprise users. *(fetched)* — [ChartMogul](https://chartmogul.com/reports/saas-conversion-report/)
- **[benchmark survey]** OpenView 2022: companies that did human outreach to users "retained them 2-3x better than those who didn't." *(fetched)* — [PR Newswire](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/openviews-third-annual-product-benchmarks-report-reveals-plg-companies-2x-more-likely-to-grow-quickly-301562090.html)

### Inferences
- The switch criteria that recur are: (1) inbound volume exceeds manual capacity; (2) the founders can state the top confusions and best practices that calls were resolving; (3) the product no longer needs a human to paper over missing functionality. None is a number.
- For a product whose install needs an engineer and often a security review, a concierge period is also the cheapest way to observe the hand-off and the review, which are otherwise invisible to analytics. A "Collison installation" for this product is a call with the buyer and their engineer on which the script goes onto staging. This is my application of the cited practice.
- Gated and self-serve are not exclusive. The pattern in the sources is a self-serve product with a human-assisted lane for larger accounts.
- Selection bias inflates every "human onboarding doubles activation" figure: Superhuman pre-qualified who received calls.

### Gaps
- No reliable data on waitlist-to-signup conversion, waitlist decay over time, or the conversion cost of a request-access form versus open signup for B2B.
- An article on Graphite's waitlist strategy (frontlines.io) returned HTTP 429 and could not be read.
- Brian Balfour's note on whether concierge onboarding scales was not fetched.
- Sacra's claim that Superhuman phased out personalised onboarding around 2023 is a single secondary source.

## 8. Third-party script trust: what buyers require before adding a DOM-reading script, and how that changes onboarding

### Takeaway
A script in the customer's app runs with the app's own privileges, so security reviewers treat it as code they are shipping. The vendor documentation I read expects customers to control loading (pinned version, CSP, optionally SRI or self-hosting), to mask sensitive content in the browser before anything leaves, and to roll out to internal users first. Since 31 March 2025, PCI DSS additionally requires every script on a payment page to be inventoried, justified and integrity-protected, which matters directly to any guide that operates on billing screens. I found no survey quantifying how often security review blocks or delays such installs.

### Cited Findings

**Why reviewers care**
- **[vendor article, security vendor]** "Third-party scripts execute at runtime with the same privileges as first-party code. They can read form fields, cookies, and session tokens, modify the DOM, and make network requests." The same vendor proposes tiering scripts by data sensitivity: tier 1 (payment fields, credentials, session tokens) reviewed on every change, low tiers annually. *(search snippet only)* — [cside, third-party script risk management framework](https://cside.com/blog/third-party-script-risk-management-framework)
- **[engineering blog]** Adobe notes that iframe isolation is a trade-off because scripts that do analytics or tag management "need access to the DOM". *(search snippet only)* — [Adobe developer blog](https://blog.developer.adobe.com/improving-third-party-javascript-security-b1745b29f75d)

**What a careful vendor documents (PostHog as the example)**
- **[docs]** "Use version controls, Subresource Integrity (SRI), Content Security Policy (CSP), and nonces to control how you load it. These controls protect different boundaries; none makes untrusted code safe to run." *(fetched)* — [PostHog docs, securely install the JavaScript SDK](https://posthog.com/docs/libraries/js/security)
- **[docs]** Pinning: "Use an exact dependency version, commit the lockfile, and enforce frozen installs in CI." Limits: "Version pins and versioned feature scripts select releases; they don't verify the bytes returned by a CDN or proxy." *(fetched)* — [PostHog docs](https://posthog.com/docs/libraries/js/security)
- **[docs]** CSP: review `script-src` and `connect-src` separately ("permitting event ingestion doesn't require permitting remote JavaScript"); test in report-only mode first; "Nonces authorize execution; they don't verify script contents or replace SRI." *(fetched)* — [PostHog docs](https://posthog.com/docs/libraries/js/security)
- **[docs]** npm bundling instead of a CDN snippet, with `disable_external_dependency_loading: true`, for customers who refuse remote code loading; a reverse proxy "doesn't verify script contents or reduce the privileges of code running on your page." "Review the release and assets through your normal dependency approval process." *(fetched)* — [PostHog docs](https://posthog.com/docs/libraries/js/security)

**PII masking**
- **[docs]** FullStory: "All excluded or masked elements are processed locally in the user's browser or mobile app." "Excluded and masked content never leaves the user's device." Masking is "the default setting for Private by Default customers". A Preview Mode lets admins "test new data capture rules on their own sessions before pushing these changes live." "If there is ever a situation where you are on the fence, go with the most private option." *(fetched)* — [FullStory help](https://help.fullstory.com/hc/en-us/articles/360020623574)
- **[docs]** Pendo: masking and blocking "are applied client side when the replay event is captured, so masked or blocked data is never sent to Pendo"; CSP settings can prevent capture and must be configured. *(search snippet only)* — [Pendo help, Session Replay privacy](https://support.pendo.io/hc/en-us/articles/18049064847515)

**Regulation that names scripts**
- **[standard, via practitioner summary]** PCI DSS v4 requirement 6.4.3 "applies to every script loaded and executed on a payment page": "a method must be in place to confirm that each script is authorised to run", "a method must assure the integrity of each script", and "a written inventory of every script must be maintained" with "a business or technical justification for why each one is necessary". Requirement 11.6.1 requires a mechanism that detects unauthorised modification of security-relevant HTTP headers and payment-page script content. Version 4.0.1 relaxed the justification wording to "business or technical". *(fetched)* — [Report URI blog](https://blog.report-uri.com/pci-dss-4-0-1-and-requirements-6-4-3-and-11-6-1-what-changed-for-payment-page-scripts/)
- **[standard, via vendor summaries]** These requirements became mandatory on 31 March 2025, and PCI SSC FAQ 1588 is cited as confirming they apply to scripts deployed by a third party as well as by the merchant. *(search snippet only)* — [Clover developer docs](https://docs.clover.com/dev/docs/pci-dss-v40-requirements-643-and-1161); [cside compliance guide](https://cside.com/blog/pci-dss-4-0-1-requirements-6-4-3-11-6-1-client-side-compliance-guide)

**How this changes onboarding (rollout patterns)**
- **[docs]** Intercom: "we recommend creating a plan for testing out Intercom internally before rolling it out to prospects and customers." "Install Intercom in production, but only for a test segment (i.e. fellow teammates/internal users)", for example with the audience rule "email address contains @companydomain.com". Then: "we recommend rolling-out Intercom to a small segment first" by page, segment or channel, "as opposed to setting it live across your entire product to all customers all at once." *(fetched)* — [Intercom help, testing and going live](https://www.intercom.com/help/en/articles/7321743-best-practices-for-testing-intercom-and-going-live)
- **[docs]** Intercom offers a separate test workspace for practice, but "There's not a way to push/migrate your messages or workflows from the test workspace to your production workspace." *(fetched)* — [Intercom help](https://www.intercom.com/help/en/articles/7321743-best-practices-for-testing-intercom-and-going-live)
- **[docs]** Least-privilege installer role: Intercom's developer invite grants "the minimum permissions required to install the Messenger". *(fetched)* — [Intercom help](https://www.intercom.com/help/en/articles/16946116-new-messenger-install-experience-on-your-website)
- **[docs]** Kill switch and staged exposure for AI behaviour: Intercom's Releases let teams "publish to everyone, ramp traffic gradually, or run an A/B test", pause a rollout, and roll back in one step. *(fetched)* — [Intercom blog, Evals and Releases](https://www.intercom.com/blog/announcing-evals-and-releases/)
- **[survey, context only]** Vanta's 2025 State of Trust report surveyed 3,500 IT and business leaders; a secondary summary says 82% believe security and compliance enhance customer trust. *(search snippet only)* — [Vanta, State of Trust](https://www.vanta.com/state-of-trust)

### Inferences
- A security review is a second, hidden onboarding funnel with its own drop-off: buyer signs up → engineer is willing → security approves → script reaches staging → script reaches production. Each is a separate measurable stage.
- The artefacts a reviewer is likely to ask for can be inferred from what mature vendors publish: what the script reads and transmits, where masking happens, exact CSP directives, a pinned or self-hostable build, a sub-processor list and DPA, and a compliance report such as SOC 2. I did not find a source that enumerates a standard checklist, so this list is inference from vendor docs.
- Product features that let a cautious customer say yes in stages follow the Intercom pattern: separate staging and production environments or keys, an allowed-domains list, show-only-to-internal-users targeting, a small-segment or percentage rollout, and a one-click off switch. Staging-first also gives the buyer the aha on their own app before the production decision.
- A guide that points at "change billing info" screens is likely to be loaded on pages near or in PCI scope. Customers under PCI DSS will need the script in their 6.4.3 inventory with a justification and an integrity method. A documented way to exclude payment pages reduces that burden. This is my inference from the requirement text.
- Intercom's inability to migrate from a test workspace to production is a known pain point; a promote-to-production path would avoid it.

### Gaps
- No survey data found on what share of mid-market or enterprise buyers require SOC 2, a security questionnaire or a DPA before installing a client-side script, or on how long such reviews delay installs. A "Trust Center reduces deal cycles by 30%" figure is a vendor claim without method.
- The PCI DSS text itself and FAQ 1588 were not read at the PCI SSC source; wording comes from practitioner summaries, and effective-date and third-party-scope statements come from vendor pages.
- Pendo's CSP page, and the security pages of Hotjar, Heap and LogRocket, were not read.
- No evidence was found on whether staged rollout features increase install rates.

## 9. Onboarding for AI-agent products (2024–2026)

### Takeaway
A common shape has emerged among AI customer-service agents: connect or train on the customer's content, test in a preview or playground, run batch tests or simulations against real past questions, then go live to a limited audience and widen. By 2025–2026 the leading vendors had added formal evaluation and release tooling. The evidence that test-before-live raises activation or trust is vendor assertion and customer quotes only; I found no measured result.

### Cited Findings
- **[vendor announcement]** Intercom, 13 August 2026, Evals and Releases for its Fin agent: "Evals tests Fin's behavior at scale using simulated scenarios built from your own conversations", each scored pass or fail against criteria the team sets. "Releases gives your team a dedicated space to build changes away from the live version of Fin." "Run an Eval against any changes within the Release before you publish it." "When a Release is ready you can publish it to everyone or run an A/B test against Fin's current configuration", or "ramp traffic gradually"; experiments report on "resolution rate, escalation rate and CSAT". Stated risk: "a 1% regression could affect thousands of conversations a day." Customer quote (AutoDS): "Now we prove it before it goes live." No adoption or trust metrics. *(fetched)* — [Intercom blog](https://www.intercom.com/blog/announcing-evals-and-releases/)
- **[docs]** Intercom's earlier layers: Preview/Test Fin as a specific audience or preview user; Batch test, which runs Fin against real customer questions "so you go live with confidence"; Simulations for Procedures, which "run the procedure in the background with no customer-facing output"; in-browser call simulation for Fin Voice. *(search snippet only)* — [Intercom help, test Fin](https://www.intercom.com/help/en/articles/10521711-test-fin-ai-agent); [Intercom changelog, batch test](https://www.intercom.com/changes/en/91745-simulate-users-and-automations-for-fin-in-batch-test-so-you-go-live-with-confidence)
- **[vendor announcement]** Decagon, 23 September 2025, Simulations: AI-generated mock-user conversations test procedure selection, knowledge retrieval, guardrails and brand tone, with pass/fail results and traces. "As a best practice, teams run simulations before go-live, after launch, and before any major update to AOPs or knowledge." Claimed shift "from 'it works most of the time' to 'we know it works'"; one enterprise anecdote, no metrics. *(fetched)* — [Decagon blog](https://decagon.ai/blog/decagon-simulations)
- **[vendor comparison, competitor-authored]** A Fin-published comparison describes Sierra as offering simulations at scale, outcome-based evaluation and regression suites before releases, and Ada as offering bulk testing of predefined scenarios. Treat as a competitor's characterisation. *(search snippet only)* — [fin.ai, Sierra vs Decagon vs Ada](https://fin.ai/learn/sierra-vs-decagon-vs-ada)
- **[vendor guides]** Staged exposure is described as normal practice: start the agent on roughly 10–20% of traffic and widen; some describe a draft-only stage on live tickets before any customer-facing reply. *(search snippet only)* — [eesel, AI support agent testing](https://www.eesel.ai/blog/ai-support-agent-testing); [Twig, how to test AI before talking to real customers](https://www.twig.so/blog/how-to-test-ai-before-talking-to-real-customers)
- **[first-party, adjacent]** AI-assisted setup has its own trust cost: DevCycle notes "AI may not do what you expect" and "AI editing code can make people nervous." *(fetched)* — [DevCycle blog](https://blog.devcycle.com/we-rebuilt-our-onboarding-around-mcp-the-result-3x-sdk-installs/)
- **[first-party experiment, adjacent]** A safe place to try the real thing before production is associated with higher activation at Twilio (Virtual Phone, part of a redesign reporting 62% better first-message activation). *(fetched)* — [Twilio blog](https://www.twilio.com/en-us/blog/developers/redesigning-twilio-onboarding-experience-whats-new)

### Inferences
- The reference flow the team likes (account → company and domain → goal → train on website → configure → dashboard with playground and checklist) matches the first half of this industry pattern: train, then test. The second half (evaluate at scale, limited rollout, monitor, roll back) is what the larger vendors added in 2025–2026 for customers who would not go live on a playground impression alone.
- For an agent that acts inside a customer's UI, the equivalent of "deploy to X% of conversations" is "show to internal users only, then to a segment or percentage of end users", and the equivalent of an eval is a saved list of the customer's own questions with pass/fail on whether the agent pointed at the right element.
- A playground on the customer's real content does two jobs in the evidence above: it is the value-before-install step (section 4) and the trust-building step (this section).

### Gaps
- **No published evidence** (experiment or survey) was found that test-before-live features increase activation, go-live rates or measured trust for AI agents. All claims are vendor statements and customer quotes.
- I could not confirm from Intercom's help centre whether a percentage-of-conversations control exists for the initial go-live; the Releases announcement says "ramp traffic gradually" without detail.
- Sierra, Ada and Zendesk were not read at primary sources.
- I did not research Crisp's own flow or publications; the description in the brief was taken as given.

## 10. Multi-stakeholder B2B onboarding: inviting teammates, roles, and team invites as an activation and retention driver

### Takeaway
The one controlled experiment I found (Sentry, 2020) shows that letting any member request an invite, and letting outsiders request to join, raised accepted invitations by 11%, 9% and 21% combined, and that invitees became active at 71–78%. Elsewhere the association between multi-user accounts and retention is asserted or correlational, and a generic "invite your team" step during signup is used by about 1% of new signups (Rows). Invites work when they are tied to a task the inviter needs done.

### Cited Findings
- **[first-party controlled experiment]** Sentry, 12 February 2020 (30-day A/B test, four equal customer segments, results described as statistically significant). Changes: invite form became an in-context modal; invite buttons were placed where a teammate is needed (suspect commit, issue assignment, team creation); "Request to Invite" let non-admins ask an admin to invite someone (previously "only roughly half of Sentry users could actually use our new modal"); "Request to Join" let outsiders ask to join an organization. Results: "11% more users accepted invitations in the Request to Invite treatment vs. the baseline"; "9% more users accepted invitations in the Request to Join treatment"; 21% more with both. "In the baseline treatment, users became active after accepting an invitation at a rate of 71.3%"; treatments ranged from 72.7% to 78.4%, best for Request to Invite. Longer-term retention was not measured. *(fetched)* — [Sentry blog](https://blog.sentry.io/how-we-grew-sentrys-monthly-active-users-by-rethinking-invitations)
- **[first-party experiment, pre/post]** Rows, 2023: an invite-teammates step in onboarding was used by "~1% of new sign-ups"; invites per signup rose to 0.03 from 0.01; average size of multi-member workspaces "rose by 14%". Background observation: larger workspaces had higher long-term retention before the test. *(fetched)* — [Rows blog](https://rows.com/blog/post/experimenting-with-questions-and-hints-in-the-user-onboarding)
- **[framework/opinion]** Elena Verna: "you need to activate teams in order to be able to sell to companies"; Miro did not count an account as activated without collaboration. *(fetched)* — [Elena Verna](https://www.elenaverna.com/p/hey-b2b-i-bet-you-are-measuring-activation)
- **[benchmark survey, secondary analysis]** For developer tools, referral and invite-driven leads were only 5% of product-driven leads (9% for freemium), below the all-SaaS baseline. *(fetched)* — [boldstart](https://boldstart.vc/?p=1596)
- **[docs]** Role-scoped invitation for the installer: Intercom's developer invite carries only install-related permissions. *(fetched)* — [Intercom help](https://www.intercom.com/help/en/articles/16946116-new-messenger-install-experience-on-your-website)
- **[first-party experiment]** Upscope's one-click email to a developer, with the vendor cc'd, is an invite tied to a specific task and is the change credited with halving non-installation. *(search snippet only)* — [Upscope blog](https://blog.upscope.com/this-simple-intercom-email-trick-helped-us-double-installations)
- **[framework/opinion]** Hulick's caution about invite steps placed for the vendor's benefit: a "spam your friends" step is "not necessarily in the user's best interest". *(fetched)* — [Intercom interview](https://www.intercom.com/blog/podcasts/user-onboarding-interview-samuel-hulick/)
- **[benchmark survey]** PQL triggers are often team-based: "As soon as you hit five users in the same account." *(fetched)* — [Amplitude blog with OpenView](https://www.amplitude.com/blog/benchmark-product-led-growth)

### Inferences
- Contrast Rows (generic invite step, about 1% use) with Sentry and Upscope (invite attached to a concrete need). The invite that matters for an engineer-installed product is "send this to the person who can install it", not "invite your team".
- At least three roles appear in the sources and deserve distinct paths: owner or buyer (configures, tests, pays), installer (needs only the snippet, environment keys and a verification screen), and approver (needs security documentation and may never log in). Sentry's "request to invite" shows the value of letting a non-admin pull in the right person without waiting for an admin.
- Invitee activation of 71–78% at Sentry suggests invited users are a high-quality cohort; whether the invited developer completed the install is a natural account-level activation signal.

### Gaps
- No rigorous public study was found quantifying the retention lift from team invites. OpenView figures that team-based products retain around 80% of paid users versus 40–60% for single-player products appeared only in a search snippet of a page that is no longer retrievable.
- A "68% versus 25% day-30 retention" figure for collaboration users circulates in secondary material with no traceable origin; excluded.
- Well-known anecdotes (Slack's 2,000 messages, Figma's collaborator invite) were not verified at primary sources and are not cited.

## 11. Synthesis for a product whose value appears only inside the customer's own logged-in app and whose install requires an engineer

### Takeaway
**Everything in this section is my inference from the findings above, not a finding in itself.** The evidence implies putting a real, pre-install experience on the customer's own content first, treating the engineer hand-off as the central designed step, expecting the largest drop between "buyer is convinced" and "script is live anywhere", and instrumenting account-level stages from the first customer, with the first cohort onboarded by hand because the most important number (install rate for buyer-not-installer products) is not published anywhere.

### Cited Findings
The claims that carry the synthesis, with their sources:
- Setup is not the aha, and B2B activation is an account-level event. — [Elena Verna](https://www.elenaverna.com/p/hey-b2b-i-bet-you-are-measuring-activation)
- Almost half of one snippet product's signups had not installed after a month, and a "can you install this yourself?" fork with a one-click developer email halved that. *(search snippet only)* — [Upscope blog](https://blog.upscope.com/this-simple-intercom-email-trick-helped-us-double-installations)
- Even with developer signups, the best case was about 70% reaching first event within two days. — [Sentry blog](https://blog.sentry.io/small-data-big-insights)
- Automating the install produced the largest reported lifts (about 3x installs; 94% versus 67% activating within an hour), without controls. — [DevCycle](https://blog.devcycle.com/we-rebuilt-our-onboarding-around-mcp-the-result-3x-sdk-installs/); [PostHog](https://posthog.com/newsletter/context-engineering)
- A safe way to try the real product before the gated step accompanied large activation gains. — [Twilio](https://www.twilio.com/en-us/blog/developers/redesigning-twilio-onboarding-experience-whats-new); [Chameleon](https://www.chameleon.io/shift-left/auditing-onboarding)
- Blocking steps work when the step is the value; optional help is skipped by about 85%. — [First Round Review, Superhuman](https://review.firstround.com/superhuman-onboarding-playbook/)
- Qualifying questions and a work-email nudge cost little conversion; ask only what you branch on. — [Rows](https://rows.com/blog/post/experimenting-with-questions-and-hints-in-the-user-onboarding); [Twilio](https://www.twilio.com/en-us/blog/developers/redesigning-twilio-onboarding-experience-whats-new)
- Checklists are completed by about 19% and touched by about 23% of users; constant progress bars do not reduce drop-off, and slow starts increase it. — [Userpilot](https://userpilot.com/blog/product-metrics-benchmark-report/); [Chameleon](https://chameleon.io/benchmark-report); [Villar et al.](https://research.google/pubs/where-am-i-a-meta-analysis-of-experiments-on-the-effects-of-progress-indicators-for-web-surveys/)
- Internal-users-first, then small-segment rollout is the documented norm for in-app scripts; AI agents add test, evaluate and gradual release. — [Intercom help](https://www.intercom.com/help/en/articles/7321743-best-practices-for-testing-intercom-and-going-live); [Intercom blog](https://www.intercom.com/blog/announcing-evals-and-releases/)
- Scripts on payment pages must be inventoried, justified and integrity-protected under PCI DSS. — [Report URI](https://blog.report-uri.com/pci-dss-4-0-1-and-requirements-6-4-3-and-11-6-1-what-changed-for-payment-page-scripts/)
- Founders who onboarded early customers by hand learned what self-serve needed to say, and switched when volume exceeded capacity. — [Arcade](https://arcade.software/post/going-self-service); [First Round Review](https://review.firstround.com/superhuman-onboarding-playbook/); [Paul Graham](https://paulgraham.com/ds.html)
- Human touch at enterprise accounts is the majority practice even in self-serve products, and outreach correlates with roughly double conversion and 2–3x retention. — [ChartMogul](https://chartmogul.com/reports/saas-conversion-report/); [boldstart](https://boldstart.vc/?p=1596); [PR Newswire, OpenView 2022](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/openviews-third-annual-product-benchmarks-report-reveals-plg-companies-2x-more-likely-to-grow-quickly-301562090.html)

### Inferences

**Order of steps (inference)**
1. *Signup*: Google or work-email sign-in, with a nudge (not a block) toward work email. Capture company and app domain.
2. *One forking question*: "Can you add a script to your app yourself?" (yes / no, someone else will / not sure). This is the Upscope and Twilio pattern, and it is the only segmentation question the evidence clearly justifies, because it changes the next screen. A goal question is optional and should be kept only if it changes defaults.
3. *Train on the customer's public content* (site, help centre) so that something real exists within minutes.
4. *Playground before install*: let the buyer ask their own questions and see answers, and if feasible see the pointer on a screenshot, a recorded session or a staging URL of their own app. This is the pre-install aha and the trust step. It should be the default landing, not a checklist item.
5. *Install hand-off as a first-class step*: for "I'll do it", stack-specific instructions, a copy-paste snippet or an AI-assistant/CLI path, and live verification ("we saw your script on staging.example.com"). For "someone else will", a shareable install link or email that needs no account or a minimum-permission installer role, with the vendor reachable on the thread, plus a short security summary for the approver.
6. *Staging first, then internal users in production, then a segment or percentage*, with an off switch. Each stage is a visible state in the dashboard.
7. *First real end-user question answered in production* is the account's activation event. Repeat usage over the following weeks is the habit signal.

**What to ask at signup (inference)**
- Email (Google sign-in available), company name, app domain, and the can-you-install fork. Possibly role, if it changes the dashboard default. Everything else (team size, goal detail, billing) is a "yellow" step in Wes Bush's terms and can be delayed. Defer email verification to before install or go-live, not before the playground.

**Where the biggest drop-off will be (inference)**
- Between "buyer has seen the playground" and "script detected on any environment". That gap contains three separate failure points: the buyer never sends the hand-off; the engineer never acts; security review stalls it. Based on Upscope's roughly 50% non-install rate for a similar buyer-not-installer product, and Sentry's roughly 70% best case with developer signups, planning for well under half of signups reaching a live install without human help is reasonable. This is an estimate from two weak data points, not a benchmark.
- A second, smaller drop is likely between staging and production because of security approval. No source quantifies it.

**What to measure from day one (inference)**
- Account-level funnel with timestamps: request or signup → content trained → first playground question → hand-off sent (and to whom) → installer opened the link → script detected on staging → script detected on production → enabled for internal users → enabled for end users → first end-user question answered → Nth question / week-4 usage.
- Time between each stage, especially hand-off sent → script detected.
- Answer to the fork question, signer's role, and installer's identity, so install rate can be split by "signer can install" versus "signer cannot".
- Whether a security review happened, what was asked for, and how long it took (collected by hand at first).
- Playground quality signals: questions asked, share judged correct.
- Checklist interaction and completion, as a secondary metric only; do not use checklist completion as the activation metric.
- With small numbers, read these as a list of named accounts, not as percentages. The benchmarks in section 2 (25–37% median activation) are an orientation, not a target, because their definitions differ.

**Gating versus self-serve for now (inference)**
- With no accounts and no dashboard built, the sources favour keeping the request-access gate for the first cohort and doing a "Collison installation" with the buyer and their engineer on a call, because the hand-off and the security review are exactly the parts that analytics will not explain. Write down each confusion and each security question; those notes are the specification for the self-serve flow. Open self-serve when inbound exceeds what can be handled by hand and the top confusions are known, keeping a human-assisted lane for larger accounts.

**On the reference design (inference)**
- The Crisp-style wizard maps well onto steps 1–4. Two adjustments follow from the evidence: add the can-you-install fork and the hand-off, which a chat-widget wizard can treat lightly but an in-app script cannot; and order the "Get Started" checklist so that the first items can be completed without an engineer, with one legitimately pre-checked item, and consider fewer than ten items since vendor guidance says three to five and data show most users never finish.

### Gaps
- The central planning number, the install-completion rate for snippet products where the buyer is not the installer, is unpublished. The first cohort's own data will be better than anything found here.
- No source tests the specific pattern recommended here (playground on the customer's content before an in-app script install) for a comparable product. It is an extrapolation from Twilio, Chameleon and the AI-agent vendors.
- No evidence was found on how much security review delays or blocks client-side script installs, or on which artefacts shorten it.
- Whether a checklist helps at all for this product type is untested in public sources.
